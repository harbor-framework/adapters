# Microbiota-derived antagonism of CD8⁺ T-cell-intrinsic androgen receptor signalling: mechanism, comparison and critical appraisal

## 1. Defining the core mechanism

### 1.1 The axis is *cell-intrinsic*, not systemic

The regulatory axis under evaluation is **antagonism of the androgen receptor (AR, *NR3C4*) expressed within the CD8⁺ T cell itself** by specific gut-microbiota-derived metabolites (principally microbially modified bile acid derivatives — e.g. 3β-hydroxy/iso- and allo-bile acids such as isoallolithocholic acid and 3β-hydroxydeoxycholic acid — that reach lymphoid tissue and tumour through the portal and systemic circulation).

This must be distinguished explicitly from **systemic androgen effects**. Androgens act on the thymus, on bone marrow progenitors, on stromal and endothelial cells, on the prostate and on skeletal muscle, and systemic castration or systemic AR blockade changes T-cell biology indirectly through all of those compartments. The axis discussed here is different in kind: the metabolite enters the T cell, binds the T cell's own AR, and reprogrammes that cell's transcriptional output. The decisive experimental discriminator is a **conditional, T-cell-restricted *Ar* deletion (e.g. *Ar*^fl/fl^;*Cd8a*-Cre or *Lck*-Cre)**, which phenocopies the metabolite in the absence of any change to circulating androgen, and which abolishes the metabolite's effect on CD8⁺ T cells while leaving systemic androgen physiology intact. Bone-marrow chimeras and adoptive transfer of *Ar*-deficient versus *Ar*-sufficient OT-I cells into the same host complete the argument.

### 1.2 Molecular mechanism: competitive occupancy of the AR ligand-binding domain

AR is a type-I nuclear receptor: in the unliganded state it is cytoplasmic, chaperoned by HSP90; agonist (testosterone/5α-dihydrotestosterone, DHT) binding to the **ligand-binding domain (LBD)** triggers helix-12 (H12) repositioning to close the agonist-bound "AF-2" surface, releases chaperone, exposes the N/C interaction (FxxLF–AF-2) and the nuclear localisation signal, and drives dimerisation, nuclear import, androgen-response-element (ARE) occupancy and recruitment of p160/SRC coactivators.

The microbial metabolites act as **competitive antagonists at the AR LBD**: their steroidal (cholane) scaffold is sufficiently similar to that of the endogenous androgen ligands to occupy the same hydrophobic LBD pocket, but their 3-hydroxyl stereochemistry and A/B-ring configuration (3β-OH, 5α/5β-allo/iso configurations) fail to stabilise the agonist H12 conformation. The consequences are (i) **competitive displacement of DHT/testosterone from the LBD**, and (ii) stabilisation of an antagonist conformation that recruits corepressors (NCoR/SMRT) rather than coactivators, so AR-dependent transcription is not merely reduced but actively repressed at AREs.

### 1.3 Functional output: preservation of the Tscm phenotype, read out by TCF-1

AR signalling in activated CD8⁺ T cells is a **differentiation-promoting, stemness-opposing** input: liganded AR occupies AREs in the regulatory landscape of effector/exhaustion genes and, critically, represses the *Tcf7* locus (directly and via AR-dependent induction of repressive programmes), thereby pushing cells away from the stem-like state and towards effector differentiation and, under chronic antigen, towards exhaustion.

Antagonising AR therefore **relieves repression of *Tcf7***, and the functional outcome is the **promotion and preservation of the stem-like memory CD8⁺ T-cell (Tscm / TCF-1⁺ progenitor-exhausted, T^PEX^) phenotype**. The key molecular correlate, and the appropriate validating read-out, is **TCF-1 (*Tcf7*) expression and activity**: AR-antagonist-exposed or *Ar*-deleted CD8⁺ T cells show increased TCF-1 protein and increased TCF-1 chromatin occupancy, coupled to the canonical stemness signature (SLAMF6^hi^, CD62L/*Sell*^hi^, *Bach2*, *Id3*, *Bcl6*, *Slamf6* up; *Tox*, *Havcr2*/TIM-3, *Entpd1*/CD39, *Prdm1*/BLIMP-1, *Id2* down), retained proliferative/self-renewal capacity on re-challenge, superior secondary expansion after serial adoptive transfer, and greater persistence in tumour.

**Core mechanism, stated compactly:** specific gut-microbial metabolites competitively bind the AR LBD in CD8⁺ T cells and block cell-intrinsic AR-driven transcription; loss of AR-mediated repression of *Tcf7* sustains TCF-1 activity, and the cell is held in — or returned towards — the self-renewing Tscm/TCF-1⁺ progenitor state rather than progressing to terminal effector/exhausted fates.

## 2. Mechanistic comparison with other pathways governing CD8⁺ stemness and exhaustion

### 2.1 Versus extrinsic IL-7/IL-15 (γc cytokine) signalling

| Feature | Intrinsic AR antagonism | Extrinsic IL-7/IL-15 |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating event** | **Intrinsic receptor *inhibition*** — ligand occupancy that *blocks* an intracellular receptor already present in the T cell | **Extrinsic receptor *activation*** — a soluble cytokine from stroma/DCs/IL-15Rα-trans-presenting cells engaging and *triggering* a surface receptor (IL-7Rα/CD127 or IL-15Rβ/CD122, each with γc) |
| **Signal transduction** | **AR-mediated (nuclear-receptor) transcription**: direct ARE occupancy, derepression of *Tcf7*, corepressor (NCoR/SMRT) recruitment; no kinase cascade required | **JAK/STAT activation**: JAK1/JAK3 → STAT5A/B phosphorylation, dimerisation and GAS-element binding, plus PI3K–AKT–mTOR and MAPK branches |
| **Transcriptional output** | Removal of an AR-imposed repressive input on the stemness network | Positive induction of *Bcl2*, *Mcl1*, *Myc*, *Tcf7*-supporting survival/self-renewal programme |
| **Mode of action** | **Removing a brake** | **Providing positive input** |
| **Dependency** | Requires only the metabolite + the T cell's own AR | Requires a cytokine *source* and adequate receptor expression — both frequently limiting in the TME |

**Critical analysis of how the two differ in contributing to Tscm maintenance.** IL-7/IL-15 maintain Tscm by *supplying* survival and self-renewal signals: STAT5 and mTOR-dependent metabolic fitness, anti-apoptotic protein induction, and homeostatic (antigen-independent) proliferation. Their effect is therefore *quantitative and supply-limited* — it scales with local cytokine availability, with IL-7Rα/CD122 surface expression (both of which are downregulated on chronically stimulated, exhausted tumour-infiltrating cells), and it can be out-competed by Tregs that consume IL-2/IL-15 and by stroma that sequesters IL-15. Pushing this axis pharmacologically (IL-15 superagonists, IL-7 agonists) also carries the liability that strong STAT5/mTOR signalling simultaneously *drives effector differentiation*, so the stemness benefit is partly self-limiting.

Intrinsic AR antagonism works in the opposite direction: it does not add signal, it **withdraws a cell-autonomous repressive input** on the stemness programme. Three consequences follow. (i) It is *not supply-limited by the cytokine milieu* — it needs no stromal cytokine source and is indifferent to IL-7Rα/CD122 downregulation. (ii) Because it works by derepression of *Tcf7* rather than by driving mTOR, it raises stemness without the coupled differentiation/effector push that limits γc-cytokine agonism. (iii) Its effect is permissive, i.e. it *raises the ceiling* on what a given amount of IL-7/IL-15 can achieve, which predicts **additivity or synergy** rather than redundancy when combined with γc-cytokine support: the cytokines supply the positive input, AR antagonism removes the transcriptional brake that otherwise caps the self-renewal programme.

### 2.2 Versus TCR/co-inhibitory-receptor-driven exhaustion

| Feature | Intrinsic AR antagonism | TCR/co-inhibitory-driven exhaustion |
|---|---|---|
| **Molecular basis** | **Modulation of AR target genes**: an acute, ligand-dependent, *reversible* change in ARE-driven transcription that **preserves stemness** by derepressing *Tcf7* | **TOX-driven epigenetic silencing**: chronic TCR signalling → sustained Ca²⁺/calcineurin–NFAT → **TOX** (and TOX2, NR4A1-3, IRF4, BATF) induction → recruitment of HBO1/KAT7 and chromatin remodellers, de novo DNMT3A-dependent methylation and closure of the *Tcf7*, *Il7r* and *Myb* regulatory regions, and stabilisation of an exhaustion-specific enhancer landscape (*Pdcd1*, *Havcr2*, *Lag3*, *Tigit*, *Entpd1*) |
| **Receptors involved** | Nuclear receptor (AR), intracellular | Surface TCR plus PD-1/CTLA-4/TIM-3/LAG-3/TIGIT, acting via SHP-1/SHP-2 and PP2A to attenuate proximal TCR signalling |
| **Durability** | Ligand-dependent and **reversible** on metabolite withdrawal; chromatin remains permissive | **Epigenetically fixed**; persists after antigen withdrawal and after checkpoint blockade; not reversed by PD-1 blockade alone |
| **Cellular outcome** | **Preserved potential**: self-renewal, multipotency, retained proliferative burst capacity, ability to give rise to new effectors | **Terminal dysfunction**: loss of proliferative capacity, reduced IL-2/TNF/IFN-γ, metabolic and mitochondrial failure, fixed hyporesponsiveness |

**Critical evaluation and implications.** These are not two settings of one dial; they are categorically different levels of regulation. AR antagonism operates at the level of **transcription-factor-dependent gene regulation on a still-plastic chromatin template** — it adjusts which AR target genes are read out, and it maintains the progenitor state from which effectors are continuously regenerated. TOX-driven exhaustion operates at the level of **heritable epigenetic state**: once the *Tcf7*/*Il7r* loci are methylated and closed and the exhaustion enhancers are established, the lesion is no longer ligand-reversible, and relieving a transcriptional brake can no longer open a locus that is epigenetically shut.

The implication for sustained anti-tumour responses is therefore directional and important: **AR antagonism is a prophylactic/upstream intervention, not a rescue of terminal exhaustion.** Its value lies in enlarging and protecting the TCF-1⁺ progenitor pool *before* and *during* chronic stimulation, so that the tumour continues to be supplied with newly differentiated effectors — the pool that is known to be responsible for the proliferative burst after PD-1 blockade and for long-term tumour control. It cannot be expected to re-animate TOX^hi^, terminally differentiated TIM-3⁺CD39⁺ cells. Quantitatively, this predicts that benefit will be greatest where progenitor cells still exist (earlier disease, lower antigen load, after lymphodepletion, in manufactured cell products) and least in heavily exhausted, high-tumour-burden settings.

### 2.3 Orthogonality, and the case for synergy with immunotherapy

Comparing all three axes together — **intrinsic AR antagonism, extrinsic IL-7/IL-15 cytokine support, and TCR/co-inhibitory-driven exhaustion** — the AR mechanism is substantially **orthogonal** to the other two:

- It uses a **different receptor class** (intracellular nuclear receptor) and a **different transduction logic** (direct ARE-level transcription/derepression) from both the γc–JAK/STAT pathway and the TCR–ITAM/ZAP70/PI3K and PD-1–SHP-2 axis. There is no shared proximal node that could make the interventions redundant.
- It is **independent of the limiting inputs of the other pathways**: it does not require cytokine availability or receptor re-expression (unlike IL-7/IL-15 agonism), and it does not require residual proximal TCR signalling competence (unlike checkpoint blockade, which only relieves inhibition of a signal that must still be delivered).
- Its output — **TCF-1⁺ stem-like cells** — is precisely the substrate on which checkpoint blockade acts.

The resulting synergy argument with **checkpoint blockade (anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4)** is mechanistically explicit: PD-1 blockade does not create progenitors, it licenses the expansion and effector differentiation of existing TCF-1⁺ T^PEX^ cells. AR antagonism supplies and protects that reservoir. Because it works by derepressing *Tcf7* on still-open chromatin, it generates a **Tscm reservoir that is comparatively resilient to the exhaustion-inducing signals of the TME** — chronic antigen, PD-L1, TGF-β, hypoxia — by keeping cells in the TOX-low, epigenetically plastic compartment rather than allowing them to transit into the TOX^hi^ fixed state. Combined, the prediction is that AR antagonism increases the *size and quality of the responding pool* while checkpoint blockade increases the *per-cell effector output of that pool*, with γc-cytokine support able to add a third, non-overlapping positive input. The corollary is also a testable falsification: in a host whose tumour-infiltrating CD8⁺ cells are uniformly TOX^hi^/TCF-1^neg^, the combination should fail — which is exactly the experiment that distinguishes genuine orthogonality from an artefact of model choice.

## 3. Critical assessment of TME interference

### 3.1 The quantitative problem: ligand competition at the AR LBD

Competitive antagonism is, by definition, a mass-action problem. Fractional AR occupancy by a microbial antagonist depends on the **relative local concentrations and relative affinities** of the competing ligands:

$$\theta_{\text{antagonist}}=\frac{[M]/K_{d}^{M}}{1+[M]/K_{d}^{M}+[A]/K_{d}^{A}}$$

where $[M]$, $K_d^M$ are the free intracellular concentration and dissociation constant of the microbial metabolite and $[A]$, $K_d^A$ those of the endogenous androgen. The numbers are unfavourable:

- **Affinity asymmetry.** DHT binds AR with sub-nanomolar affinity ($K_d \approx 0.1$–$1$ nM); testosterone is low-nanomolar. Microbially derived bile-acid-type AR ligands are, by contrast, typically **micromolar-range** binders. A 10³–10⁴-fold affinity deficit must be overcome by concentration alone.
- **Concentration asymmetry and compartmentalisation.** Systemic testosterone in males is ~10–30 nM with free (non-SHBG-bound) fractions of ~1–2%, but the relevant variable is the *intratumoural* androgen concentration, which is set by local steroidogenesis and intracrine conversion (AKR1C3, HSD17B3, SRD5A1/2 converting adrenal precursors and testosterone to DHT) and can exceed plasma levels. Conversely, the microbial metabolite must survive hepatic first-pass extraction, enterohepatic recycling and conjugation, be transported into the tumour (which is often poorly perfused and acidic), and then cross the T-cell membrane in sufficient free concentration. Free intracellular metabolite concentration — not faecal or portal concentration — is what competes at the LBD, and the gradient from gut lumen (high µM–mM) to intratumoural T-cell cytosol can be several orders of magnitude.
- **Consequence.** Even generous estimates suggest the metabolites may achieve only partial AR occupancy in an androgen-replete, intracrine-competent TME, especially in male hosts and in tumours with active local androgen synthesis. Formally, the required metabolite concentration for half-maximal blockade scales as $K_d^M(1+[A]/K_d^A)$, i.e. it rises linearly with local androgen tone — so any tumour that upregulates intracrine androgen production directly titrates the therapy away. This argues that endogenous metabolites are more plausibly *modulators* of AR tone than *blockers* of it, and that pharmacological potency optimisation (or combination with an androgen-synthesis inhibitor) would be needed for reliable effect.

### 3.2 The qualitative problem: functionally dominant, AR-independent immunosuppression

Even complete AR blockade in tumour-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells may yield no clinical benefit, because the TME contains **independent, parallel and frequently dominant** suppressive mechanisms that act downstream of, or orthogonally to, the stemness programme. Three concrete examples:

- **Adenosine signalling (CD39/ENTPD1–CD73/NT5E–A2AR).** ATP released from dying tumour cells is hydrolysed by CD39 and CD73 to adenosine, which engages the Gs-coupled **A2A receptor** on CD8⁺ T cells, raising cAMP and activating PKA–CREB. This directly suppresses proximal TCR signalling, IL-2 and IFN-γ production, and cytotoxic granule release, and it does so *regardless of whether the cell is TCF-1⁺ and stem-like*. A perfectly preserved Tscm pool that differentiates into effectors inside an adenosine-rich tumour will produce effectors that are functionally muted. Adenosine additionally stabilises Tregs and M2 macrophages, compounding the problem.
- **IDO1/TDO–kynurenine activity.** IDO1 expressed by tumour cells and DCs depletes tryptophan and generates kynurenines. Tryptophan depletion activates GCN2 and inhibits mTORC1 in T cells, arresting proliferation, while kynurenine is an **AHR** agonist that promotes Treg and tolerogenic DC differentiation. Both are nutrient/ligand-level constraints that cap effector output irrespective of AR occupancy; indeed kynurenine-driven AHR signalling imposes its own transcriptional programme that can override a derepressed *Tcf7* locus.
- **Hypoxia and HIF-1α.** Tumour hypoxia (<1% O₂) drives HIF-1α-dependent glycolytic rewiring, lactate and acid accumulation (which inhibits T-cell MCT-mediated lactate export and thus glycolysis), PD-L1 and VEGF upregulation, impaired mitochondrial fitness and mitochondrial ROS-driven terminal exhaustion. Hypoxia also limits drug and metabolite penetration into the tumour core, simultaneously worsening the quantitative problem in §3.1.

**Critical conclusion.** The AR axis governs *differentiation state*; adenosine, IDO/kynurenine and hypoxia govern *acute functional competence and metabolic capacity*. Preserving stemness is necessary but demonstrably insufficient: if the terminal effector step is blocked by any of these dominant pathways, the net anti-tumour benefit can be nil despite full target engagement. This predicts that the axis will only show benefit in combination (e.g. with A2AR/CD73 blockade, IDO inhibition, or vascular normalisation), and it warns against interpreting negative monotherapy results as disproof of the mechanism.

## 4. Critical evaluation of metabolite specificity and nuclear-receptor cross-talk

### 4.1 Structural basis for cross-talk

The nuclear receptor superfamily shares a **highly conserved LBD fold**: a three-layered, antiparallel **α-helical sandwich of 11–12 helices (H1–H12) plus a short β-turn**, enclosing a buried hydrophobic ligand pocket, with the AF-2 coactivator groove formed by H3, H4/H5 and H12. Agonist-induced H12 repositioning ("mousetrap") is a common allosteric mechanism across the family. Two features make cross-reactivity structurally unavoidable for steroid-like ligands:

1. **A common ligand chemotype.** AR, GR (*NR3C1*), MR, PR, ER (*NR3A1/2*), and the bile-acid/sterol-sensing receptors FXR (*NR1H4*), PXR (*NR1I2*), LXRα/β (*NR1H3/2*), VDR and CAR all accept rigid, fused-ring, four-ring (steroid/cholane/secosteroid) scaffolds. A microbially modified bile acid is, chemically, a steroid; it is *a priori* a candidate ligand for every one of these pockets.
2. **Pocket promiscuity and size.** PXR in particular has an unusually large, flexible, polymorphic pocket that accommodates a remarkable chemical range; FXR is the physiological bile-acid receptor and binds 3-, 6- and 7-hydroxylated cholanes with high affinity; the NR3C subgroup (AR/GR/MR/PR) pockets are mutually similar enough that cross-binding between androgens, progestins and glucocorticoids is a classical pharmacological problem (e.g. spironolactone at AR/PR, antiandrogens at GR/PR). Consequently, discriminating AR from FXR/PXR/LXR/GR/ER requires exploiting subtle stereochemical determinants (3β- vs 3α-OH, 5α- vs 5β-A-ring fusion, side-chain length and charge) rather than gross scaffold differences — a narrow and fragile selectivity margin.

### 4.2 A mechanistically detailed detrimental off-target consequence

**Off-target agonism of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR, *NR3C1*) in CD8⁺ T cells and in the TME.** GR shares the closest LBD architecture with AR within NR3C, so a cholane antagonist designed for AR has a realistic probability of GR engagement. If the metabolite behaves as a GR agonist in T cells, the consequences would directly oppose the therapeutic intent:

1. Ligand-bound GR translocates and transactivates *TSC22D3* (**GILZ**), *FKBP5*, *KLF13*, *DUSP1* and *BCL2L11*, and trans-represses AP-1/NF-κB-dependent loci.
2. GILZ sequesters c-Jun/c-Fos and p65 and inhibits RAF/MEK/ERK signalling; DUSP1 dephosphorylates ERK/JNK/p38. Proximal TCR-to-MAPK signal transmission is therefore blunted.
3. GR signalling suppresses *Il2*, *Ifng*, *Tnf* and *Prf1*/*Gzmb*, induces *Pdcd1*, and drives BIM-dependent apoptosis of activated T cells.
4. In parallel, GR activation in DCs and macrophages induces a tolerogenic, IL-10^hi^/IL-12^lo^ phenotype and favours Treg induction.

The **net result would be iatrogenic immunosuppression**: a drug given to expand a stem-like CD8⁺ reservoir would simultaneously apoptose activated effectors, mute cytokine output and tolerise antigen-presenting cells — potentially converting a positive signal in a reductionist *in vitro* assay into a *negative* effect on tumour control *in vivo*, and precisely the kind of discordance that would make a clinical programme uninterpretable. Clinically this is not hypothetical: glucocorticoid exposure is a well-documented negative predictor of checkpoint-blockade efficacy.

Other concrete off-target liabilities, with mechanism: **FXR agonism** would induce *NR0B2*/SHP and *FGF19*, repress CYP7A1 and alter the entire bile-acid pool (a feed-forward perturbation of the very metabolite axis being manipulated), while FXR activation in NKT and myeloid cells is anti-inflammatory and, in hepatocytes, pruritogenic and dyslipidaemic. **PXR agonism** would induce CYP3A4, CYP2B6 and ABCB1/P-gp, accelerating the clearance of co-administered chemotherapy, kinase inhibitors and the metabolite itself — producing dose-dependent, self-limiting pharmacokinetics and serious drug–drug interactions. **LXR agonism** would induce SREBP-1c-driven hepatic lipogenesis and hypertriglyceridaemia, and ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux changes that alter T-cell membrane cholesterol and TCR nanoclustering. **ER agonism** would cause feminising endocrine effects and, in ER⁺ malignancies, direct tumour-promoting signalling. Each of these could counteract the desired anti-tumour effect or add independent toxicity.

## 5. Critical evaluation of the therapeutic production and delivery challenge

### 5.1 The metabolites require specific, multi-step microbial enzymatic pathways

Generating the relevant AR-antagonistic bile-acid derivatives from host-conjugated primary bile acids is not a single reaction; it is an **obligate multi-step, multi-organism pathway**:

1. **Bile salt hydrolases (BSH)** of *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, *Clostridium*, *Bacteroides* and *Enterococcus* deconjugate taurine/glycine from primary bile acids — a mandatory first step, since conjugated species are not substrates for downstream modification.
2. **Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDHs)** — specifically **3α-HSDH, 3β-HSDH, 7α-HSDH and 12α-HSDH** (e.g. in *Clostridium scindens*, *Eggerthella lenta*, *Ruminococcus gnavus*, *Clostridium* and *Bacteroides* spp.) — perform the stereochemical epimerisation at C-3 (and oxidation/reduction at C-7/C-12) that creates the **iso- (3β-OH)** configuration essential for AR LBD antagonism. These are the pivotal, rate- and specificity-determining enzymes, and HSDH activity is typically distributed across *two* organisms (one oxidising, one reducing the 3-oxo intermediate), so the full epimerisation is a *cross-feeding* reaction requiring co-occurrence of distinct taxa.
3. **Additional enzyme classes are indispensable:**
   - **5α/5β-reductases (steroid-5-reductases; the *baiCD*/*baiH* NADH:flavin oxidoreductases)**, which set the A-ring *allo* (5α-H) versus normal (5β-H) stereochemistry that distinguishes isoallo- from iso-species and largely determines nuclear-receptor selectivity;
   - **bile-acid 7α-dehydratase (BaiE)** and the rest of the multi-gene *bai* operon (*baiB*, *baiA*, *baiCD*, *baiF*, *baiH*), which carry out 7α-dehydroxylation — the step restricted to a very small number of low-abundance *Clostridium* cluster XIVa organisms;
   - **bile-acid CoA ligases/transferases** and **ene-reductases** that activate and process the intermediates.

So a single bioactive species requires **deconjugation → oxidation → stereospecific reduction → dehydration/dehydroxylation**, catalysed by at least three distinct enzyme classes (**HSDHs plus reductases plus dehydratases/ligases**), frequently in *different* bacterial species, in a defined sequence, in a defined gut region.

### 5.2 Why this collides with the ecology of the microbiome

The microbiome is **ecologically variable and intrinsically unstable**:

- **Inter-individual variability.** The taxa carrying 7α-dehydroxylation (*C. scindens*-like organisms) are present in only a subset of individuals and typically at <0.1% relative abundance; HSDH gene content varies by orders of magnitude between individuals, and functional (not just taxonomic) capacity varies further because of strain-level gene-content differences. Diet, geography, age, host genotype and bile-acid pool composition all shift it.
- **Intra-individual instability.** The same person's bile-acid-modifying capacity changes with diet (fat and fibre intake directly change bile flow and substrate supply), diurnal and feeding rhythms, transit time, pH, proton-pump inhibitors, and — decisively in oncology — with **antibiotics, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and opioids**, all of which are near-universal in cancer patients and can collapse the low-abundance *bai*-positive and HSDH-positive populations within days.
- **Cross-feeding fragility.** Because the pathway is distributed across organisms, loss of any single step abolishes the end product; the system has little functional redundancy at the 7α-dehydroxylation and 3β-epimerisation steps.
- **Hepatic and enterohepatic confounding.** Even when produced, the metabolites are subject to host re-conjugation (BAAT), sulfation (SULT2A1), glucuronidation and ASBT/OSTα-β/NTCP-dependent recirculation, so colonic production is only loosely coupled to systemic free concentration.

### 5.3 Why this is a *fundamental* obstacle to consistent dosing by endogenous manipulation

Therapeutics require a defined, reproducible exposure–response relationship. Attempting to achieve the effect by **endogenous manipulation — diet, prebiotics, probiotics, FMT or engineered consortia — makes the "dose" the emergent output of a multi-species biochemical network inside a variable ecosystem**, and this fails the basic requirements of drug development on several counts simultaneously:

- **No controllable dose.** The input (a probiotic strain or a dietary substrate) is separated from the active moiety by several enzymatic steps performed by organisms whose abundance the clinician cannot set; there is no monotonic, titratable input–output relation, and no way to dose-escalate in an individual patient.
- **No reliable engraftment.** Introduced strains colonise unpredictably against an established community and resident niche competition, so the rate-limiting enzyme may simply never be supplied.
- **Unacceptable inter- and intra-patient variability.** Exposure would vary by orders of magnitude between patients and over time within a patient, guaranteeing that a fraction of patients are sub-therapeutic and confounding any attempt to establish a therapeutic window (already narrow for the reasons in §6) or to interpret a randomised trial.
- **Vulnerability to standard co-medication.** Antibiotics given for neutropenic fever would abolish the therapy mid-course — an operationally intolerable property.
- **No pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic anchor.** Faecal metabolite levels are a poor surrogate for the free intracellular concentration at the AR LBD in a tumour-infiltrating T cell, so even therapeutic drug monitoring is not straightforward.

**Critical conclusion:** the biology may be real, but *endogenous* delivery is not a viable therapeutic modality. The rational translational path is to abandon microbial production and instead use the metabolite as a **chemical starting point for a synthetic, orally bioavailable, T-cell-penetrant, AR-selective small molecule** (or to engineer the receptor-side intervention directly — e.g. *AR* knockout or AR-degrader exposure in *ex vivo*-manufactured CAR-T/TIL products, where dose and exposure are fully controlled and systemic AR is untouched).

## 6. Rigorous evaluation of systemic toxicity risk and the therapeutic window

### 6.1 Physiological systems dependent on AR signalling

Systemic AR blockade is not a tolerable "free" intervention, because AR is a pleiotropic receptor with major, non-redundant roles in several organ systems:

- **Reproductive system (the dominant liability).** AR is essential for spermatogenesis (Sertoli-cell AR is required for meiotic progression and spermiogenesis), for Leydig-cell function and the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal feedback loop, for the prostate, seminal vesicles and epididymis, and for libido and erectile function. Systemic AR antagonism therefore causes hypogonadal symptoms, oligo/azoospermia and **infertility** (potentially irreversible with prolonged exposure), gynaecomastia, and loss of libido and sexual function; in women, AR contributes to ovarian follicular development and to libido.
- **Musculoskeletal system.** AR drives skeletal muscle protein synthesis and satellite-cell function and is required for the maintenance of bone mass (directly, and indirectly through aromatisation to oestrogen). Blockade produces **sarcopenia, loss of lean mass, fatigue and reduced physical function, osteopenia/osteoporosis and fracture risk** — effects that are particularly damaging in cancer patients already at risk of cachexia, and that themselves worsen performance status and tolerance of other therapy.
- **Cardiovascular and metabolic systems.** Androgen deprivation is associated with adverse metabolic remodelling — insulin resistance, increased visceral adiposity, dyslipidaemia — and with increased cardiovascular morbidity; AR also has direct effects on vascular tone and cardiomyocyte function. Hot flushes, anaemia (loss of androgen-driven erythropoiesis) and mood/cognitive effects (AR in the CNS) complete the recognised toxicity profile of clinical androgen-axis blockade.

### 6.2 The therapeutic window problem

Establishing a clinically viable window is genuinely difficult, for structural reasons:

- **The target is the same receptor in both compartments.** Unlike an immune-checkpoint antibody, there is no tumour- or lineage-restricted epitope here: the molecule that blocks AR in a tumour-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cell blocks the identical receptor in Sertoli cells, myocytes, osteoblasts and the CNS. Selectivity must be achieved by *distribution*, not by *target*, which is a far weaker lever.
- **Compartmental pharmacokinetics work against the intended site.** To reach free intracellular concentrations at the AR LBD inside a T cell within a poorly perfused, hypoxic, high-interstitial-pressure tumour that are sufficient to out-compete locally synthesised DHT (§3.1), the systemic concentration must be high — and the systemic compartment is exactly where muscle, bone, gonad and CNS AR sit, with far better perfusion. The exposure gradient is therefore inverted relative to what is needed: **systemic AR blockade is achieved at doses *below* those that achieve adequate intra-tumoural immune modulation.** This is the central argument that **dose-limiting toxicity (hypogonadism, sarcopenia, bone loss, metabolic/cardiovascular effects) will be reached before sufficient intra-tumoural AR occupancy is attained**, especially given the µM-range potency of the natural metabolites and the sub-nM potency of the competing endogenous agonist.
- **Chronic dosing is required.** Because maintaining a Tscm reservoir is a continuous, ligand-dependent process (§2.2 — the effect is reversible), exposure must be sustained over months, which converts tolerable acute effects into cumulative ones (fracture risk, irreversible infertility, cardiovascular events) and removes the option of brief, high-exposure pulses.
- **The population at risk is sex- and disease-dependent.** The window differs between male and female patients, and is especially problematic in younger patients for whom fertility preservation matters, and in prostate cancer where AR-axis blockade is already standard-of-care (confounding attribution) while in other tumours it adds entirely new toxicity.
- **No validated pharmacodynamic biomarker of the intended effect.** The measurable systemic read-outs (testosterone, LH, PSA) report the *toxicity* arm, not the intra-tumoural immune arm; without a tumour-based TCF-1/Tscm read-out (requiring serial biopsies), dose-finding has no handle on the therapeutic side of the ratio.

**Mitigation strategies that follow from this analysis:** restrict AR blockade to the *ex vivo* manufacturing phase of adoptive cell products (genetic *Ar* deletion or transient AR degradation in the product, with no systemic exposure at all); use tumour-targeted delivery or T-cell-targeted conjugates/nanoparticles; use selective AR modulators with tissue-selective (muscle- and bone-sparing) profiles; or use intermittent scheduling timed to the checkpoint-blockade-driven proliferative burst. Each narrows, but none abolishes, the problem.

## 7. Synthesis across the immune network: AR antagonism is not CD8-exclusive

AR is expressed, and functional, in many immune lineages; a systemically delivered AR antagonist will act on all of them simultaneously, so the net effect on tumour immunity is a *network* outcome rather than a CD8⁺ T-cell outcome.

- **Regulatory T cells (Tregs).** AR signalling influences FOXP3⁺ Treg induction and stability, and androgen tone has well-documented sex-dimorphic effects on Treg frequency and on *Foxp3* locus regulation; androgens generally favour a more tolerogenic, Treg-permissive state, and AR also feeds into the TGF-β/SMAD and mTOR-dependent circuits that control peripheral Treg differentiation. The directionality is genuinely uncertain and may be context-dependent: **if AR antagonism destabilises intratumoural Tregs**, it would amplify the CD8⁺ benefit (reduced IL-2 consumption, reduced CTLA-4-mediated co-stimulation stripping, reduced TGF-β and IL-10, reduced CD39/CD73-derived adenosine). **If instead it increases Treg induction or stability** — equally plausible, given AR's repressive effects on some tolerogenic programmes and the possibility that TCF-1 derepression also favours Treg persistence — it would directly oppose it, blunting or cancelling the intended effect.
- **Myeloid lineage: tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) and MDSCs.** AR is expressed in monocytes, macrophages and granulocytic/monocytic MDSCs, where it modulates polarisation and suppressive capacity. Androgen signalling has been linked to the suppressive, pro-tumour M2-like/immunosuppressive myeloid programme and to MDSC expansion and arginase/iNOS/ROS-dependent suppression; AR antagonism could therefore reduce MDSC suppressive output and repolarise TAMs towards an M1-like, IL-12-producing, antigen-presenting phenotype — a strongly favourable, CD8-supporting effect. But myeloid AR also restrains NF-κB-dependent inflammatory output, so blockade could equally drive excessive IL-6/IL-1β/TNF and myeloid-derived chronic inflammation, which promotes angiogenesis, tissue remodelling, tumour progression and T-cell dysfunction, and risks cytokine-mediated toxicity.
- **Other populations (further complexity).** AR signalling affects neutrophils (NETs and T-cell suppression), NK-cell cytotoxicity and maturation, B-cell development and autoantibody risk, thymic output (androgens drive thymic involution; AR blockade enhances thymopoiesis and could change the TCR repertoire reaching the tumour), and DC maturation and cross-presentation capacity.

### Critical assessment of the net outcome

The combined effect is therefore **non-linear and potentially non-monotonic**, for three reasons:

1. **Opposing vectors.** The CD8⁺ (pro-tumour-control) and Treg (possibly pro-tumour) effects can subtract; the myeloid effect can add or subtract depending on polarisation outcome. The net sign is the sum of terms whose individual signs are not yet established in the same model system.
2. **Non-linear coupling.** These populations are coupled by shared, saturable resources and feedback loops — IL-2 and IL-15 consumption, adenosine production by CD39⁺/CD73⁺ Tregs and myeloid cells (§3.2), TGF-β, and antigen availability. A change in one compartment changes the input to the others, so network outputs need not scale with AR occupancy, and dose–response curves may be bell-shaped (benefit at partial occupancy, loss of benefit at full occupancy if Treg- or inflammation-promoting effects have higher thresholds).
3. **Plausible net negation.** It is entirely possible for a drug to produce a textbook-positive CD8⁺ phenotype (more TCF-1⁺ Tscm, better *ex vivo* recall capacity) while the host-level outcome is neutral or worse — e.g. if simultaneous Treg stabilisation and myeloid-driven chronic inflammation dominate, or if enhanced thymic output of naive cells dilutes tumour-specific clones. **The CD8⁺ T-cell benefit can therefore be negated by effects on the rest of the immune network.**

**Experimental implication.** Distinguishing these possibilities requires the cell-type-restricted genetics set out in §1.1, applied lineage by lineage: *Ar*^fl/fl^ crossed to *Cd8a*-Cre, *Foxp3*-Cre, *LysM*-Cre and *Cd11c*-Cre in the same tumour models, with mixed chimeras and adoptive transfers to separate cell-intrinsic from network effects, and with explicit comparison of pharmacological (whole-body) blockade against CD8-restricted deletion. Only if the pharmacological effect approximates the CD8-restricted genetic effect is the simple model tenable.

## 8. Integrated conclusion: weighing potential against limitations

**The potential is real and mechanistically well-motivated.** The axis identifies a cell-intrinsic, ligand-controlled, chromatin-permissive brake on CD8⁺ T-cell stemness, operating through a druggable nuclear receptor and read out by an unambiguous molecular correlate (TCF-1). Because it is **orthogonal** to γc-cytokine (JAK/STAT) support and to TCR/co-inhibitory exhaustion, and because it acts upstream of TOX-driven epigenetic fixation, it offers something the current armamentarium lacks: a way to *enlarge and protect the TCF-1⁺ T^PEX^/Tscm reservoir* that checkpoint blockade and adoptive cell therapy depend on but do not create. The clearest therapeutic proposition is **immunotherapy potentiation** — enhancing Tscm to supply and sustain the proliferative burst after PD-1 blockade, or to improve the persistence and polyfunctionality of manufactured TIL/CAR-T products.

**That potential must, however, be weighed against limitations that are severe and, in one case, arguably disqualifying for the most-discussed delivery route.**

- **Microbial variability and delivery is the single largest obstacle.** The active metabolites are the end product of an obligate multi-step pathway requiring **HSDHs** (3α/3β/7α-HSDH) *together with* **5α/5β-reductases and 7α-dehydratase (BaiE)/CoA ligases**, distributed across low-abundance, cross-feeding taxa in an ecosystem that varies by orders of magnitude between individuals and is routinely destroyed by the antibiotics, chemotherapy and radiotherapy that oncology patients receive. Endogenous manipulation (diet, probiotics, FMT, engineered consortia) therefore cannot deliver a controllable, titratable, reproducible dose, cannot support dose escalation or PK/PD monitoring, and cannot survive standard co-medication. **The axis is better viewed as a biological discovery that supplies a chemical and genetic starting point — a synthetic, T-cell-penetrant, AR-selective small molecule, or receptor-side genetic editing in *ex vivo* cell products — than as a microbiome-based therapy.** Weighed against the Tscm benefit, this limitation determines the modality: the benefit is worth pursuing, but not by the endogenous route.
- **Systemic toxicity narrows the window to the point where monotherapy at efficacious systemic exposure is doubtful.** AR is non-redundant in the **reproductive** system (spermatogenesis, fertility, libido) and in the **musculoskeletal** (and cardiovascular/metabolic) systems, and the perfusion and potency arithmetic predicts dose-limiting hypogonadism, sarcopenia and bone loss before adequate intra-tumoural AR occupancy is reached — compounded by the need for chronic exposure and by the absence of a tumour-side pharmacodynamic biomarker. This argues strongly for compartment-restricted strategies (*ex vivo* editing; targeted delivery; tissue-selective modulators; intermittent scheduling).
- **TME interference can neutralise even complete target engagement.** Quantitatively, µM-affinity microbial ligands must out-compete sub-nM DHT whose local concentration is set by intracrine tumour steroidogenesis; qualitatively, **adenosine (CD39–CD73–A2AR), IDO1–kynurenine–AHR and hypoxia–HIF-1α** impose dominant, AR-independent constraints on effector function and drug penetration. Preserved stemness is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and combination with these axes is a prerequisite rather than an optimisation.
- **Specificity and network effects add further risk.** The conserved NR LBD fold makes off-target engagement of **GR, FXR, PXR, LXR or ER** a realistic liability with concrete, mechanism-based harms (GR-driven GILZ/DUSP1-dependent immunosuppression and T-cell apoptosis being the most directly self-defeating; PXR-driven CYP3A4 induction the most disruptive pharmacologically), while simultaneous AR blockade in **Tregs** and **macrophages/MDSCs** makes the net host-level outcome non-linear and capable of negating the CD8⁺ benefit entirely.

**Net judgement.** On balance, the microbiota–AR–CD8⁺ T-cell axis is of **high biological significance and genuine but conditional therapeutic promise**. Its significance lies in revealing nuclear-receptor-level, microbially tuneable control of T-cell stemness — a new regulatory layer, orthogonal to cytokine and checkpoint biology, and a plausible contributor to the sex differences and microbiome-dependence of immunotherapy response. Its therapeutic promise is credible only if the mechanism is divorced from its microbial origin and from systemic exposure: the rational programme is (i) chemically optimised, AR-selective ligands or AR degraders with verified selectivity against GR/FXR/PXR/LXR/ER, deployed (ii) in a compartment-restricted way — preferentially during *ex vivo* manufacture of adoptive cell products, or with tumour/T-cell-targeted delivery — and (iii) in combination with checkpoint blockade plus neutralisation of a dominant TME axis (A2AR/CD73, IDO or hypoxia), with (iv) lineage-restricted genetic experiments establishing the net network effect before clinical translation, and (v) tumour-based TCF-1/Tscm pharmacodynamics to anchor dose-finding. Pursued as a microbiome intervention delivered systemically at AR-blocking exposures, by contrast, the approach is very likely to founder on dosing irreproducibility, endocrine toxicity and TME-imposed futility.
