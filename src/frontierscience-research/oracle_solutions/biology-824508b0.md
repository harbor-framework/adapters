# *waslb*/*Wasl* and the fin-to-limb transition

## 1. Gain-of-function models of *waslb* in zebrafish

Transgenic over-expression / activating (gain-of-function, GOF) manipulation of *waslb* in the zebrafish pectoral fin does not produce a globally bigger or smaller fin; the effect is **specific to the endoskeletal radials**, i.e. to the cartilage elements that lie between the endoskeletal disc and the dermal fin rays.

Reasoning: *waslb* acts in the chondrogenic/joint-forming compartment of the appendage, not in the ectodermal fin-fold programme or in the dermal skeleton. One therefore predicts, and observes, a change in the *number and arrangement of cartilage elements* rather than in the fin's overall outline.

Specifically:

- **Fin development is altered within the radials.** The perturbation is restricted to the radial (endochondral) skeleton of the pectoral fin.
- **The normal teleost pectoral fin has only a proximal row and a distal row of radials.** In *waslb* GOF animals, **supernumerary "intermediate" radials appear between the normal proximal and distal radial rows**, i.e. an extra, interposed row of elements is intercalated between the two normal rows. This is effectively the addition of a new round of segmentation/joint formation along the proximodistal axis.
- **The fin fold is unaffected.** The apical ectodermal fold and the overall fin outline develop normally.
- **The fin rays (dermal lepidotrichia) are unaffected.** Ray number, branching and segmentation are normal.

**Final answer (1):** *waslb* gain-of-function adds extra, intermediate radial cartilages between the normal proximal and distal radial rows of the pectoral fin endoskeleton, while leaving the fin fold and the dermal fin rays normal.

## 2. Loss-of-function models of *waslb* in zebrafish

The reciprocal experiment cannot be read out at the level of fin patterning, because *waslb* is required much earlier and much more broadly than the fin:

- ***waslb* loss-of-function (null knockout) is embryonically lethal in zebrafish.** Mutant embryos do not survive to the stages at which pectoral-fin endoskeletal patterning (radial segmentation, ray formation) can be scored.

Consequently the fin-patterning requirement for *waslb* cannot be assessed in a straight germline null; conditional, mosaic, hypomorphic or late-acting (e.g. temporally controlled knockdown / fin-specific) approaches are required, and the informative zebrafish data on fin pattern therefore come from the gain-of-function side.

**Final answer (2):** *waslb* knockouts are embryonically lethal, so there is no scorable fin-patterning phenotype from germline loss-of-function; the gene's fin-patterning role in zebrafish is revealed by gain-of-function rather than by loss-of-function.

## 3. Conditional knockout of *Wasl* in mice: limb malformations, and other models with the same phenotypes

Because the mouse null is also lethal, the limb role of *Wasl* is uncovered with a **limb-/mesenchyme-specific conditional knockout**.

### The malformations

- **Malformations of the autopod (hands and feet), and occasionally of the zeugopod (radius/ulna in the forelimb, tibia/fibula in the hindlimb).** The stylopod (humerus/femur) is comparatively spared, so the defect is an intermediate-to-distal limb defect.
- **Lack of joint segmentation in the digits** — the phalangeal elements fail to be divided by interzones, giving unsegmented, rod-like or fused digits (symphalangism-like).
- **Reduced cartilage formation** — the chondrogenic condensations are smaller and cartilage matrix/element size is diminished.

### Other transgenic limb models sharing these malformations

The *Wasl* conditional phenotype is not unique; it converges on several classic limb-patterning mutants:

- **Distal *HOX* mutants — *Hox11*–*Hox13* group (e.g. *Hoxa11/Hoxd11*, *Hoxa13/Hoxd13*) mutants.** These give precisely the zeugopod + autopod (intermediate/distal) reduction and element-identity loss that *Wasl* cKO limbs show; the phenotype closely resembles distal *HOX* mutations.
- ***Gdf5* mutants (brachypodism).** The *failure of joint formation/segmentation* is shared with *Gdf5* loss-of-function, in which interzone specification and joint cavitation fail and digit elements fuse.
- **AER/*Fgf8* loss (e.g. conditional *Fgf8* inactivation in the AER).** Loss of distal signalling at later stages of limb patterning produces the distal (autopod, and with *Fgf4/Fgf8* compound loss also zeugopodal) truncations/hypoplasia seen here.
- ***Wnt9a* knockdown/knockout.** The reduction in joint formation and failure of joint maintenance partially resembles *Wnt9a* (formerly *Wnt14*) loss-of-function, in which joint interzones are induced but not maintained and elements fuse.

**Final answer (3):** *Wasl* conditional mutant mice show autopod (and occasionally zeugopod) malformations, loss of digit joint segmentation and reduced cartilage; the phenotype closely resembles distal *HOX* (*HOX11*–*HOX13*) mutants, *GDF5* mutants (joint fusion), AER/*FGF-8* loss (distal structures) and *WNT9A* knockdown (reduced joint formation).

## 4. Role of *wasla* in pectoral fin patterning and outgrowth

The zebrafish genome contains two *WASL* co-orthologues, *wasla* and *waslb*, retained from the teleost genome duplication. Paralogue retention does not imply shared function: after duplication, one copy frequently keeps (or acquires) the appendage function while the other is sub-functionalised to other tissues.

For *wasla*:

- **There is no strong evidence that *wasla* plays a major role in zebrafish fin development.** It is not required for pectoral fin outgrowth or for radial/ray patterning, and its manipulation does not reproduce the radial phenotypes seen with *waslb*. The appendage-relevant functions in zebrafish are carried by ***waslb***.

**Final answer (4):** No — *wasla* has no demonstrated major role in pectoral fin patterning or outgrowth; the fin-relevant paralogue is *waslb*.

## 5. Comparing *waslb* gain-of-function in zebrafish with *Wasl* knockout in mice

### The comparison itself

- **Both manipulations converge on the same developmental process: joint formation/segmentation of the endoskeleton.** In zebrafish, raising *waslb* activity *adds* a row of segmented radial elements (extra joints/interzones); in mouse, removing *Wasl* activity *abolishes* digit joint segmentation. The two experiments are mirror images acting on one shared process.
- **The opposing effects of the two experiments suggest that the necessity of *wasl* activity in the appendage is itself correlated with the fin-to-limb transition.** In the teleost fin, *waslb* is not *required* for normal pattern (extra activity adds elements); in the tetrapod limb, *Wasl* is *required* (its loss destroys pattern). The gene has therefore shifted from a permissive/modulatory role to an essential one in the lineage leading to tetrapods.
- **In both taxa the changes fall in the intermediate and distal part of the appendage skeleton** — intermediate radials in the fish fin, zeugopod/autopod in the mouse limb — highlighting the importance of this gene in *later* appendage patterning and elaboration rather than in the initiation of the appendage bud or in proximal (stylopod/girdle) elements.

### Relationship to the fin-to-limb transition

- **Joint formation is the functional crux of a weight-bearing limb.** A terrestrial, weight-bearing appendage requires discrete, segmented, articulated elements (elbow/knee, wrist/ankle, digit joints) that can transmit load and generate propulsive motion against a substrate; a fin, loaded hydrodynamically and not in compression, does not. A gene whose activity sets the number and position of joints is therefore exactly the kind of gene expected to be recruited during the fin-to-limb transition.
- **Expansion of *waslb*/*Wasl* activity in the distal appendage was plausibly a key step in that transition.** Increasing *waslb* activity in a fish fin is sufficient to intercalate a new row of segmented endoskeletal elements — i.e. to make the fin skeleton more "limb-like" by adding proximodistal segments and articulations. Upregulation/expansion of this activity is therefore a candidate mechanism for the stepwise addition of zeugopodal and autopodal segments.
- **The gene is essential in tetrapods but not in fishes.** The mouse requires *Wasl* for normal autopod/zeugopod joint segmentation and cartilage, whereas the zebrafish fin is patterned normally without extra *waslb* input. This asymmetry of *necessity* — dispensable for fin pattern, essential for limb pattern — is the clearest developmental-genetic signature that the gene was co-opted into an essential role as the limb evolved.

**Final answer (5):** Both manipulations specifically target joint formation in the intermediate/distal appendage skeleton, and their opposing outcomes (gain of joints in fish, loss of joints in mouse) indicate that *wasl* became *necessary* for appendage patterning only in the tetrapod lineage — tying expanded *waslb*/*Wasl* activity, and the joint formation needed for weight-bearing locomotion, to the fin-to-limb transition.

## 6. How *Vav2* interacts with *Waslb* in zebrafish

*Vav2* does not act on *waslb* directly; it works through a small GTPase intermediary:

- **Vav2 interacts with *waslb* through the protein Cdc42.**
- **Cdc42 is a G-protein (a member of the G-protein/GPCR-type signalling family) that communicates with Rac and with the ARP2/3 complex.** Vav2, acting as an exchange factor, loads Cdc42; active Cdc42 (together with Rac) then engages WASL/N-WASP, which relieves the autoinhibited conformation of the WASL protein and allows its VCA region to activate **ARP2/3**-dependent actin nucleation and branching.

So the pathway is:

$$\textbf{Vav2} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Cdc42 (with Rac)} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Waslb (N-WASP)} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{ARP2/3} \;\rightarrow\; \text{branched actin / cytoskeletal remodelling in the appendage mesenchyme}$$

This cytoskeletal output is what links *waslb* to condensation, cell-shape change and interzone/joint formation in the developing appendage.

**Final answer (6):** Vav2 signals to Waslb via **Cdc42**, a G-protein that also communicates with Rac and with the ARP2/3 complex; Cdc42 activates Waslb, which in turn activates ARP2/3-driven actin nucleation.

## 7. Hypothesised role of *waslb* in sarcopterygian evolution

### Fossil tetrapodomorphs: *Eusthenopteron* vs *Tiktaalik*/*Acanthostega*

- **The shift in *waslb* activity most likely occurred within tetrapodomorphs** — i.e. along the stem leading from fish-like sarcopterygians to digited tetrapods — rather than at the base of Sarcopterygii or within actinopterygians.
- **Early taxa probably retained a more fish-like *waslb* expression/activity pattern.** *Eusthenopteron*, with a well-developed metapterygial axis, long dermal lepidotrichia and relatively few, elongate endoskeletal elements with limited distal segmentation, is consistent with a low, fish-like level of *waslb* activity restricted to a modest number of proximodistal segments.
- **Later taxa have more tetrapod-like appendages, potentially indicating expanded *waslb* function.** *Tiktaalik* (with a differentiated "shoulder–elbow–wrist" series, multiple distal radials and a mobile, weight-bearing proximal joint) and *Acanthostega* (with a true digited autopod and reduced dermal fin skeleton) show precisely the additional distal elements and articulations that elevated/expanded *waslb* activity produces experimentally. The predicted trajectory is therefore a progressive expansion of the *waslb* domain into the distal appendage, with a concomitant increase in the number of segmented, jointed endoskeletal elements, and the acquisition of an absolute requirement for the gene (as in mouse) somewhere along this stem.

Testable prediction: the degree of distal endoskeletal segmentation in successive tetrapodomorphs should track, monotonically, the inferred level/extent of *waslb* activity, with the loss of the dermal fin skeleton (lepidotrichia) being independent of it — consistent with the fin rays and fin fold being unaffected in the zebrafish gain-of-function experiment.

### *Waslb* activity in coelacanth

- **Given the morphology of coelacanth (*Latimeria*) fins, they most likely use only a small amount of *waslb* activity in their fins.** Their lobed fins contain a segmented but modest endoskeletal series with few joints and a large dermal ray component, i.e. the morphology expected from low/fish-like *waslb* input, not from the expanded activity inferred for digited tetrapodomorphs.
- **Coelacanths have also reduced the fin endoskeleton down to the metapterygial region, giving a skeleton that is similar in appearance to that of some elpistostegalians.** This is an important caveat: superficial "limb-likeness" in *Latimeria* reflects metapterygial reduction/concentration rather than the tetrapod-style addition of distal segments and joints, so morphological resemblance to elpistostegalians should **not** be read as evidence for a tetrapod-like level of *waslb* activity. The coelacanth therefore represents a convergent, low-*waslb* route to a lobe-finned appendage.

**Final answer (7):** *waslb* is hypothesised to have been progressively expanded in the distal appendage within tetrapodomorphs — fish-like, low activity in *Eusthenopteron*, expanded activity producing additional segmented, jointed distal elements in *Tiktaalik* and *Acanthostega* — and to have become essential only in the tetrapod lineage; coelacanths, whose fins are reduced to a metapterygial skeleton that merely resembles that of some elpistostegalians, probably retain only a small amount of *waslb* activity in their fins.
