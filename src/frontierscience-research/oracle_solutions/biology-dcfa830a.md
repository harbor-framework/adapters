# Spatial and temporal control of appendage development: somatopleure commitment, HOX codes, retinoic acid, and median fins

## 1. Rotation of the HH11 somatopleure

**Experiment.** At **HH11 (~2 dpc)**, around the onset of somitogenesis, the **somatopleure** (the somatic layer of the lateral plate mesoderm together with its overlying ectoderm) is dissected out, **rotated** (reversing its anteroposterior and/or dorsoventral orientation) and grafted back into its original position. At this stage there is no limb bud, no AER, and no morphological sign of a limb field.

**Reasoning.** The key question is whether the somatopleure at HH11 is still *naive* (so that rotation would be corrected by surrounding positional cues, i.e. the tissue would be *specified* late, or regulate) or already *committed* (so that rotation carries limb-forming information into the wrong place and cannot be rescued). The outcome resolves this:

**Likely outcome.**

- **Although the limbs are not yet visible on the embryo, the cells of the somatopleure are already committed to their participation in limb development.** Limb-field identity — which cells will contribute to forelimb, interlimb flank or hindlimb — is already laid down in the somatopleure before any bud appears. The rotated tissue therefore carries its own prior positional/limb-field identity with it, rather than reading a fresh position from its new surroundings.
- **Experimental modification of the HH11 somatopleure results in over half of the embryos developing limb malformations or transformations.** The majority (>50%) of operated embryos are affected: limb buds form in abnormal positions or with abnormal axial polarity, elements are reduced, duplicated or mis-specified, and limb identity can be *transformed* (for example forelimb-type elements appearing in flank/interlimb positions or elements adopting the character of the adjacent axial level). A minority of embryos escape with normal limbs, which is why the result is a high-penetrance but not absolute phenotype.
- **Despite the early stage, cell fate is already specified and there is little compensatory function available to "fix" these perturbations.** The embryo does not regulate: there is no substantial repatterning of the rotated tissue by neighbouring signals, no compensatory respecification from adjacent somatopleure, and no significant regeneration of the displaced field. Once rotated, the mis-positioned determinative information is read out as a malformed or transformed limb.

**Final answer (1):** The rotated somatopleure retains its original limb-field identity, so most operated embryos (over half) develop limb malformations or transformations — demonstrating that even before the limb bud is visible at HH11, the somatopleural cells are already committed to their limb-developmental fate and that the embryo has little compensatory capacity to correct the perturbation.

## 2. Earliest stages to which forelimb, hindlimb and interlimb lineages can be traced, and the HOX genes involved

Fate mapping (vital dye, GFP/DiI labelling and quail–chick chimeric grafting of defined epiblast/primitive-streak territories) pushes the origin of limb-field lineages back to gastrulation — much earlier than the appearance of the limb bud — and each region is marked by a distinct HOX input:

- **Forelimb.** Forelimb-specific cells can be traced back to **HH4–HH5 (~1 dpc)**, i.e. to the full-length primitive streak/head-process stage. At this earliest stage the prospective forelimb territory is already distinguishable, and it is receptive to — and identified by — **HOXB4 and HOXB7** specifically.
- **Interlimb (flank).** The interlimb region is specified **slightly later, at HH6–HH7 (~1.25–1.5 dpc)**, and its identity is determined by **HOX9** (the *Hox9* paralogue group), which specifies the flank/interlimb territory between the two limb fields.
- **Hindlimb.** Hindlimb cells are determined at **HH8–HH9 (end of day 1 post-conception)**, and the prospective hindlimb territory is specifically targeted by **HOXB6–HOXB9**.

The temporal order (forelimb → interlimb → hindlimb) follows the anterior-to-posterior progression of gastrulation and of the collinear activation of the *Hox* clusters: more anterior lineages ingress and acquire their *Hox* code first.

**Final answer (2):** Forelimb lineages trace to **HH4–5 (1 dpc)** and are marked by **HOXB4 and HOXB7**; the interlimb region is specified slightly later at **HH6–7 (1.25–1.5 dpc)** and is determined by **HOX9**; hindlimb lineages are determined at **HH8–9 (end of day 1 post-conception)** and are targeted specifically by **HOXB6–HOXB9**.

## 3. Effect of HOXB5 deletion on limb development in mice

**Role of HOXB5.** *Hoxb5* is expressed in the anterior paraxial and lateral plate mesoderm with an anterior expression boundary at the **cervico-thoracic (cervical/thoracic) transition**, and it is this boundary that positions the **forelimb field** along the body axis. **HOXB5 is therefore involved in the specification of the forelimb at the cervico-thoracic boundary**, acting as one of the axial-level determinants that tells the lateral plate where along the anteroposterior axis a forelimb should form.

**Consequence of deletion.** Removing *Hoxb5* shifts that boundary, and with it the position of the forelimb field:

- **Deletion of *HOXB5* in mice causes the forelimb to shift anteriorly/cranially.** The forelimb field — and therefore the limb and its girdle — is displaced towards the head, i.e. rostrally, relative to the vertebral column, so that the limb attaches at a more anterior axial level than normal. **The direction of the shift is anterior (cranial).**
- **The malformations are concentrated in the pectoral girdle — the scapula and clavicle.** Mutants show scapular defects (notably abnormalities of the cranial/anterior border and of the supraspinous region of the scapula, with rostral displacement of the scapula) and clavicular defects, consistent with the cranial shift of the forelimb field, while the distal limb (humerus, radius/ulna, autopod) is comparatively normal.

So the phenotype is a **positional/identity shift of the forelimb and its girdle, not a loss of limb outgrowth**: the limb forms, but at the wrong axial level, and its girdle elements — the structures most directly derived from, and most sensitive to, the position of the cervico-thoracic boundary — are malformed.

**Final answer (3):** HOXB5 specifies the forelimb at the cervico-thoracic boundary; its deletion in mice **shifts the forelimb anteriorly/cranially**, producing malformations of the **scapula and clavicle** (pectoral girdle), with the shift occurring in the **anterior/cranial** direction.

## 4. CYP26A1 overexpression during gastrulation, and the converse experiment with exogenous RA

### 4a. Overexpression of CYP26A1

**Mechanism.** **Retinoic acid (RA) is critical for limb field specification.** RA, synthesised from retinaldehyde by RALDH2 (ALDH1A2) in the posterior/trunk mesoderm, forms a posterior-to-anterior gradient that acts through RAR/RXR heterodimers to establish anterior trunk identity, to set *Hox* expression boundaries (including the *Hox4/5*–*Hox9* boundaries used in Parts 2 and 3) and to induce the forelimb field by activating *Tbx5* (via *Hox*-dependent inputs) in the anterior lateral plate. **CYP26A1 is the cytochrome P450 enzyme that degrades RA** (4-hydroxylation of all-*trans*-RA and onward oxidation), and it normally sculpts the RA gradient by clearing RA from the anterior/rostral and from specific domains.

**Prediction.** **Overexpression of CYP26A1 during gastrulation would decrease the amount of RA available for signalling** — in effect producing a phenocopy of vitamin-A deficiency or of a *Raldh2* loss-of-function, with globally reduced RAR activation at the time the limb fields are being specified.

**Expected consequences for forelimb and hindlimb patterning:**

- With RA signalling reduced, the anterior *Hox* boundaries and the anterior limit of *Tbx5* induction are displaced caudally, so the **forelimb field shifts posteriorly**, and the loss of a sharp RA gradient means the **limb field can also become expanded/diffuse** (poorly delimited anteriorly and posteriorly) rather than crisply bounded.
- The **forelimb is poorly formed as a result of underexpression of RA**: reduced *Tbx5*/*Fgf10*–*Fgf8* feedback initiation gives hypoplastic, truncated or (in strong reductions) absent forelimbs, with proximal (girdle/stylopod) elements most severely affected. The forelimb is far more RA-dependent than the hindlimb, because hindlimb initiation runs principally through *Tbx4*/*Pitx1* and is comparatively RA-independent; the **hindlimb is therefore much less affected**, showing at most a mild positional shift and milder reduction, so the net result is an increased, abnormal forelimb–hindlimb asymmetry.

### 4b. Treatment of the embryo with exogenous RA

This is the reciprocal manipulation — raising, rather than lowering, RA availability — and it produces the **opposite phenotype to CYP26A1 overexpression**:

- **The limb shifts anteriorly.** Excess RA displaces the anterior *Hox* and *Tbx5* boundaries rostrally, moving the limb field (and the limb) towards the head — the mirror image of the posterior shift caused by RA depletion.
- **Ectopic limb formation may occur.** RA is sufficient, in competent flank/interlimb tissue, to induce a supernumerary limb bud: application of RA to the interlimb flank induces ectopic *Tbx5*/*Fgf10*/*Shh* expression and an ectopic (often mirror-image, posteriorly polarised) limb. Exogenous RA also causes digit duplications/mirror-image digit patterning when applied to the bud itself.
- Explicitly, therefore, **RA treatment gives the opposite phenotype to CYP26A1 overexpression**: anterior shift and ectopic limbs with excess RA, versus posterior shift/expanded field and poorly formed (hypoplastic) limbs with reduced RA.

**Final answer (4):** CYP26A1 overexpression degrades RA and so reduces RA signalling at the time of limb-field specification — RA being critical for limb field specification — shifting the limb **posteriorly** and/or **expanding the limb field**, and leaving the limb (especially the forelimb) **poorly formed because of RA underexpression**. Treatment with exogenous RA produces the **opposite phenotype**: the limb **shifts anteriorly** and **ectopic limbs may form**.

## 5. HOX9 at HH17 in the chick: which limb-patterning protein is specifically affected?

**Stage logic first.** The timing is decisive. At **HH4–HH9** the *Hox* genes (including *Hox9*) act in the lateral plate to set **limb-field position and identity**, i.e. the forelimb-versus-interlimb-versus-hindlimb decision, read out through **TBX5** (forelimb) and **TBX4/PITX1** (hindlimb). By **HH17**, however, the limb buds have already been initiated and specified: *Tbx5*/*Tbx4* induction is complete, the AER is forming, and the bud is engaged in **axis patterning and outgrowth**. Deleting *Hox9* at HH17 is therefore *too late* to affect field specification, so the answer is not about limb-field identity.

At this stage the relevant *Hox9* function is in the posterior limb-bud mesenchyme, where it operates in the **zone of polarising activity (ZPA) pathway**:

- **At HH17, HOX9 promotes SHH signalling via HAND2.** The *Hox9*/*Hox* 5′ paralogues act together with **HAND2 (dHAND)** in the posterior bud mesenchyme to establish and maintain the ZPA: HOX9 and HAND2 cooperatively bind and activate the limb-specific **ZRS (ZPA regulatory sequence) enhancer of *Shh***, and HAND2 is required to restrict and drive *Shh* expression posteriorly (while GLI3/ALX4 restrict it from the anterior). Loss of *Hox9* at this stage therefore reduces **HAND2**-dependent activation of the ZRS and consequently **impairs SHH expression and signalling** in the posterior limb bud.

**Consequence:** reduced SHH output degrades anteroposterior patterning of the limb — loss or reduction of posterior digits, loss of posterior zeugopodal identity (ulna/fibula), reduced SHH–GREM1–FGF (AER) feedback loop activity and hence reduced distal outgrowth.

**Explicitly ruling out the earlier function:** at HH17 an answer invoking **forelimb/hindlimb specification, or the TBX genes (TBX5/TBX4) and PITX1, is incorrect**, because those processes occur at the much earlier stages (HH4–HH9) at which *Hox9* sets limb-field identity in the lateral plate, and they are already completed by HH17.

**Final answer (5):** At HH17, deletion of *HOX9* would specifically impair **SHH (sonic hedgehog)** signalling in the posterior limb bud, acting **via HAND2** on the *Shh* ZRS enhancer — not limb-field specification or TBX gene expression, which belong to an earlier developmental window.

## 6. The germ-layer origin of fish median fins and its implications for the evolution of appendages

### 6a. Germ-layer/mesodermal origin

Lineage tracing in zebrafish and other teleosts (and in basal actinopterygians and chondrichthyans) shows that the mesenchyme of the **median (unpaired) fins — dorsal, anal and caudal — is derived from the somites, i.e. from paraxial (somitic) mesoderm**, specifically from the somitic dermomyotome/sclerotome-derived migratory cells that populate the median fin folds. This contrasts with the **paired** appendages (pectoral/pelvic fins and tetrapod limbs), whose skeletogenic mesenchyme derives from **lateral plate mesoderm** (with only muscle precursors coming from the somites).

**Final answer (6a): median fins arise from paraxial (somitic) mesoderm** — and no other germ-layer/mesodermal origin is correct for the median fin mesenchyme.

### 6b. How this informs hypotheses of early fin evolution

- **Paraxial/somitic mesoderm is evolutionarily younger than lateral plate mesoderm.** The lateral plate is the phylogenetically older mesodermal subdivision, with segmented paraxial (somitic) mesoderm being a later elaboration in the chordate/vertebrate lineage. The germ-layer origin of an appendage therefore carries phylogenetic information, and the fact that median fins are built from the *younger* tissue is itself a datum requiring explanation.
- **Hypotheses on the origin of appendages are typically tested by looking to basal taxa for data.** Because the relevant transitions are deep, inference relies on the development and morphology of basal and early-diverging taxa — lampreys and hagfish (which possess median fins but no paired appendages), chondrichthyans (sharks, skates), basal actinopterygians (*Polypterus*, sturgeon, paddlefish), lungfish and coelacanth, plus the fossil record of early agnathans and placoderms — rather than on teleosts or tetrapods alone.
- **The fin-fold hypothesis states that a singular, continuous unpaired (median) fin fold gave rise to the unpaired fins and, eventually, to the paired appendages.** In this classical scenario, a continuous median fin fold running around the body was progressively subdivided and regionalised to produce the discrete dorsal, anal and caudal fins, and lateral extensions of the same fold were likewise subdivided to generate the paired pectoral and pelvic fins — so paired appendages are, developmentally and evolutionarily, derivatives of a median-fin-like developmental programme. (The competing gill-arch/Gegenbaur hypothesis instead derives paired fins from branchial arch elements.)
- **The finding that median fins arise from paraxial mesoderm in modern taxa supports this hypothesis.** The critical evidence is that median fins are the *developmentally primary* appendage type: they appear first in phylogeny (present in jawless vertebrates that lack paired fins) and they deploy the full appendage developmental toolkit (see Part 7) from the somitic mesoderm. If the median fin programme is the ancestral appendage programme, then the paired appendages represent a *redeployment* of that programme into a new tissue — the lateral plate — which is exactly what the fin-fold hypothesis predicts: the same genetic circuitry (FGF, SHH, WNT, HOX, an apical ectodermal fold) operating first in median fins and subsequently co-opted laterally. The contrast in germ-layer origin (paraxial for median, lateral plate for paired) therefore marks the *tissue* co-option event, while the conserved signalling programme marks the *genetic* continuity between the two appendage classes.

**Final answer (6b):** Median fins arise from paraxial/somitic mesoderm — a mesodermal subdivision that is evolutionarily younger than the lateral plate. Since hypotheses of appendage origin are tested against data from basal taxa, and since the **fin-fold hypothesis** holds that a single continuous unpaired fin fold gave rise to the unpaired fins and eventually to the paired appendages, the paraxial origin and full appendage-patterning toolkit of median fins in modern taxa **supports the fin-fold hypothesis**, with the paired appendages representing redeployment of an ancestral median-fin programme into lateral plate mesoderm.

## 7. Signalling pathways shared with paired appendages, and the difference in developmental axes

### 7a. Shared signalling pathways

Median fins deploy essentially the same signalling toolkit as paired appendages and limbs:

- **Median fins express FGFs and SHH, as expected for a developing appendage.** FGF signalling (*fgf8*, *fgf24*, *fgf10*, *fgf4*) drives outgrowth and maintains the mesenchyme–ectoderm feedback loop, and *shh* (with *ptc1*/*gli* read-outs) patterns the fin and its skeletal elements and interacts with the FGF loop, just as the SHH–GREM1–FGF loop does in the limb bud.
- **Median fins also form an AEF (apical ectodermal fold)**, a thickened, pseudostratified ectodermal structure at the distal margin of the fin that is the **structural and functional counterpart of the AER (apical ectodermal ridge)** of the limb/paired fin: it expresses the FGFs, is required for continued proximodistal outgrowth, and engages in the same reciprocal signalling with the underlying mesenchyme.
- **Median fins express WNT7 (wnt7a/wnt7aa), which in the limb is the canonical marker of dorsoventral signalling.** The crucial difference is in its *spatial distribution*: **in the median fin, WNT7 is expressed throughout the entirety of the fin ectoderm, whereas in the limb it is restricted to the dorsal ectoderm only.** In the limb, dorsal *Wnt7a* induces *Lmx1b* in the dorsal mesenchyme while ventral *En1* represses *Wnt7a* ventrally, producing the dorsoventral asymmetry; in the median fin there is no such restriction, and the ligand is uniformly distributed.
- Other shared components: *hoxd* cluster expression, *tbx18*/*tbx* family inputs, BMP signalling in the fin fold and in skeletogenesis, and *and1*/*and2* (actinodin) expression in the fin fold ectoderm.

### 7b. The major difference in axes of development, and its consequences

**The major difference is that the median fin lacks a dorsoventral (DV) axis.** A median fin is a **midline, bilaterally symmetric sagittal structure**: it projects in the dorsal or ventral midline and its two faces are left and right, mirror images of each other. It therefore has only two polarised axes — proximodistal (base to fin tip) and anteroposterior (rostral to caudal along the body) — and the third axis is a plane of mirror symmetry rather than a polarised axis. A paired fin or limb, by contrast, projects laterally and possesses a genuine, polarised **DV axis** (dorsal/extensor versus ventral/flexor surfaces), controlled by the *Wnt7a*/*Lmx1b* (dorsal) versus *En1*/BMP (ventral) system, and its proximodistal and anteroposterior axes are organised relative to the body wall rather than relative to the midline.

**Consequences and interpretation:**

- **There is a lack of a DV axis in median fins, yet WNT7A signalling is present.** This is the informative dissociation: the ligand that in the limb *instructs* dorsal identity is expressed in the median fin, but without the dorsal restriction and without a DV axis to pattern. WNT7 therefore cannot be functioning as a DV determinant here; it must be performing an earlier/ancestral role (supporting fold formation, ectodermal maintenance, outgrowth and/or AP patterning) that has been *subsequently co-opted* as the dorsalising signal in paired appendages.
- **The dorsoventral axis may therefore be evolutionarily younger than the other axes of appendage development.** The proximodistal (FGF/AEF–AER) and anteroposterior (SHH) systems are present and functioning in median fins, which are the ancestral appendage type (Part 6), whereas the DV system is absent — implying that PD and AP patterning are the older axes and that the DV axis was added later, at or after the origin of laterally projecting paired appendages.
- **An apical ectodermal fold forms in the absence of ventral signalling.** The median fin builds its AEF and sustains outgrowth without the *En1*-dependent ventral ectodermal compartment and without a dorsal/ventral ectodermal boundary — whereas in the limb the AER is positioned precisely at the dorsoventral ectodermal boundary and its formation and maintenance depend on *En1*/BMP-dependent ventral ectoderm and on the dorsal–ventral interface. So the AEF/AER-forming mechanism is separable from, and more ancient than, the DV compartment system.
- **This suggests that the ventralising signals are "novel" relative to SHH and FGF activity.** The ventral programme (*En1*, BMP-dependent ventral ectoderm specification, and the consequent restriction of *Wnt7a* to dorsal ectoderm) is an evolutionary innovation layered on top of a pre-existing, ancestral FGF (outgrowth) plus SHH (anteroposterior) core. The appendage was therefore built axis by axis: an ancestral PD + AP module operating in a midline fin, to which a third, DV axis was added by the invention of ventralising signals and the spatial restriction of an already-present WNT7 ligand.

**Predicted practical consequence for experiments:** manipulations that scramble DV identity in a mouse or chick limb (ectopic ventral *Wnt7a*, loss of *En1*, dorsalisation/ventralisation experiments) have severe effects on limb pattern — bidorsal or biventral limbs, altered AER position, altered digit and tendon/muscle pattern — whereas in a median fin the equivalent manipulations should have little or no effect on pattern, because the fin has no DV axis to disturb and already expresses WNT7 throughout its ectoderm. Conversely, FGF- and SHH-directed manipulations should be comparably disruptive in both, since those axes are shared and ancestral.

**Final answer (7):** Median fins share the appendage toolkit — **FGFs and SHH**, an **apical ectodermal fold (AEF)** equivalent to the limb's AER, and **WNT7** expression (but throughout the whole fin ectoderm, unlike the dorsal-only *Wnt7a* of the limb). The major axial difference is that median fins, being midline structures, **lack a dorsoventral axis** while retaining WNT7A signalling; the AEF forms **without ventral signalling**. This implies that the **DV axis is evolutionarily younger** than the proximodistal and anteroposterior axes, and that the **ventralising signals are novel innovations relative to the ancestral SHH and FGF activities** — so median fin development proceeds on two polarised axes where limb development in mouse or chick requires three.
