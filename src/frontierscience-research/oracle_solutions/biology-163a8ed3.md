# Why telomerase inhibitors fail in ALT-dependent cancers, and a concurrent dual-TMM combination strategy

---

# Part I. Molecular basis for the inefficacy of telomerase inhibitors in ALT-reliant cancers

## 1.1 What imetelstat actually inhibits — and why that target is absent in ALT

Imetelstat (GRN163L) is a 13-mer *N*3′→P5′ thio-phosphoramidate lipid-conjugated oligonucleotide that is **not** an antisense agent but a direct, competitive **template antagonist**: it base-pairs with the template region of the telomerase RNA component (hTR/*TERC*, nt 46–58), occluding the active site of the holoenzyme and blocking the addition of TTAGGG repeats by TERT. Its target is therefore the catalytic activity of the ribonucleoprotein **TERT–hTR** complex. (Imetelstat is FDA-approved as Rytelo for transfusion-dependent anaemia in lower-risk MDS, which establishes its clinical pharmacology but also underlines that its activity requires an active telomerase enzyme.)

In ALT cells, telomere elongation is executed by a **completely different, telomerase-independent machinery**: a homologous-recombination (HR)-based, break-induced-replication (BIR) process in which one telomere uses another telomere (or extrachromosomal telomeric repeat DNA, C-circles/t-circles) as a template. The core dependencies are RAD52, RAD51/RAD51AP1–UAF1, the BLM and SLX4 nucleases, SMC5/6–NSMCE2 (MMS21)-dependent SUMOylation, the POLD3/POLD4 subunits of Pol δ, PCNA–RFC and the non-canonical RFD (recombination-dependent replication) apparatus, all assembled in **ALT-associated PML bodies (APBs)** that cluster telomeres with PML/SUMO, TRF1/TRF2, RPA, BRCA1 and the recombination machinery.

Consequences for drug action:

* **There is no catalytic site for imetelstat to occupy.** ALT cells typically have little or no TERT expression and no detectable telomerase activity by TRAP; hTR may be absent altogether. Pharmacologically, the target engagement term is zero, so the drug is inert against telomere maintenance however high the exposure.
* **Even in TERT-expressing cells, imetelstat's mechanism is intrinsically slow.** It does not kill; it merely stops telomere addition, so cells must undergo many divisions until telomeres erode to the critical length that triggers crisis (a "lag phase" of weeks to months). This long latency is precisely the window in which resistance mechanisms — including ALT induction — can be selected.
* **Cell-cycle/structural indifference of ALT.** ALT telomere elongation is not confined to a telomerase-recruitment step at S phase via the TPP1 TEL-patch/CST–shelterin hand-off, so the agents that disrupt telomerase recruitment (e.g. TPP1-TEL patch or POT1 interface) are equally ineffective.

## 1.2 The role of TERT promoter mutations (C228T/C250T): they define the telomerase-dependent population, and their absence flags ALT

The recurrent *TERT* promoter mutations **C228T (−124 C>T) and C250T (−146 C>T)**, counted from the ATG, are among the most common non-coding mutations in human cancer (~70–80% of melanomas, ~80% of glioblastomas, >70% of bladder cancers, hepatocellular carcinoma, thyroid cancer).

**Mechanism: these mutations create *de novo* ETS transcription-factor binding motifs that drive TERT overexpression.** Each single C>T transition converts the local sequence into a canonical **ETS/TCF consensus element containing a new GGAA(T) core** — i.e. a binding site that does not exist in the wild-type promoter. The newly generated motif is bound by **ETS-family factors, principally the obligate multimeric GA-binding protein GABP (GABPA with GABPB1/GABPB1L)**, whose tetramerisation allows it to bridge the new motif to native ETS sites in the promoter and to the proximal promoter/CpG island. This has several measurable consequences:

1. **Monoallelic, mutant-specific recruitment of GABPA/GABPB1** (and in some contexts ETS1/2 or GABP-dependent long-range looping to the *TERT* enhancer region), converting an essentially silent promoter into an actively transcribed one.
2. **Reactivation and overexpression of TERT** — typically a 2- to 4-fold increase in *TERT* mRNA from the mutant allele, sufficient to restore telomerase activity and immortalise the clone; recruitment of the Ser5-phosphorylated Pol II machinery, and a switch of the mutant allele's chromatin from H3K27me3-repressed to active (H3K4me2/3, loss of repressive marks), with allele-specific hypomethylation of the mutant allele.
3. **Clinical utility as a biomarker**: C228T/C250T positivity identifies the **telomerase-dependent** TMM, which is the population in which imetelstat has a mechanistic rationale, and is associated with aggressive phenotypes (e.g. glioma, melanoma, bladder cancer).
4. **Why this explains imetelstat failure in ALT:** TERT promoter mutations and ALT markers are **largely mutually exclusive**. ALT tumours (ATRX/DAXX-mutant) are overwhelmingly TERT-promoter wild type and TERT-low. So in an ALT tumour the entire GABP→TERT axis that imetelstat ultimately depends on is simply not operating: there is no mutation-driven TERT overexpression to suppress. Conversely, within a heterogeneous tumour the C228T/C250T-positive subclones are the imetelstat-sensitive compartment, and treating with imetelstat alone **selects against them and for the ATRX/DAXX-null, ALT-competent subclones**. A further nuance: GABP-driven TERT expression can also be re-established in rare resistant clones by promoter-independent mechanisms (amplification, structural rearrangement that juxtaposes enhancers, or *TERT* hypermethylation of the THOR region), so even the telomerase arm can escape a single-agent template antagonist.

## 1.3 ALT-specific chromatin remodelling: ATRX/DAXX loss and histone H3.3

**ATRX–DAXX is the replication-independent chaperone complex that deposits histone variant H3.3 into heterochromatin**, including pericentromeric, rDNA and **telomeric chromatin** (ATRX is an SWI/SNF-family ATPase that recognises G-rich/G-quadruplex and heterochromatic templates via its ADD domain reading H3K9me3; DAXX is the H3.3-specific histone chaperone).

**Loss of ATRX and/or DAXX — by truncating mutation, deletion or loss of expression (and equivalently by the H3.3 mutations G34W/G34R and K27M that cripple the pathway) — prevents the incorporation of H3.3 into telomeric chromatin, and this is what enables recombination.** The consequences, which together constitute "ALT-specific chromatin remodelling", are:

1. **Failure of H3.3 deposition at telomeres** leaves telomeric chromatin improperly assembled and depleted of its normal H3.3-containing nucleosome complement; nucleosome occupancy and heterochromatin integrity (H3K9me3/HP1, and the macroH2A/H3.3 balance) are degraded, so the telomere becomes **decompacted, nucleosome-poor and permissive**. Note the diagnostic duality used clinically: ATRX/DAXX loss is read out as **loss of the H3.3-deposition pathway at telomeres**, and in ALT tumours H3.3 itself (*H3F3A*) is a recurrent mutational target — H3.3-pathway disruption, by either route, is the shared lesion.
2. **Persistence of G-quadruplexes and R-loops (TERRA-DNA hybrids).** ATRX normally resolves/suppresses G4 structures and, with the RNA-binding activity of ATRX and the MRN complex, limits TERRA-driven R-loops. Without ATRX, G4s and R-loops accumulate at telomeres.
3. **Replication-fork stalling and collapse** on this structured, under-chromatinised template generates single-ended double-strand breaks and persistent RPA-coated ssDNA — i.e. chronic **ATR-activating replication stress** at telomeres.
4. **Recombinogenic telomeres.** The combination of exposed ssDNA, defective heterochromatin, loss of the ATRX-dependent suppression of sister-telomere exchange, and failure to load the normal shelterin/chromatin barrier **licenses HR/BIR between telomeres**: telomere-sister chromatid exchange (T-SCE) rises, APBs form (PML-dependent clustering that raises the local concentration of telomeres and recombination factors, effectively a liquid-liquid-phase-separated reaction crucible), and RAD52/RAD51AP1-dependent strand invasion with POLD3/POLD4-dependent BIR extends the telomere using another telomere as template. The outputs are the ALT hallmarks: **ultrabright, heterogeneous telomere FISH foci; very long and highly heterogeneous telomere lengths; abundant extrachromosomal C-circles; APBs**.
5. **Therapeutic implication:** this machinery contains no telomerase component, so the entire ALT elongation cycle is **pharmacologically orthogonal to imetelstat**. But it is not undruggable — it creates the HR dependency and the replication-stress dependency exploited in Part II.

## 1.4 Homologous-recombination pathways: the ALT dependency (and the reason single-agent telomerase inhibition fails)

ALT is "HR run on telomeres". Formally it is a **BIR** reaction: a one-ended DSB at a telomere undergoes RAD51-dependent (and, importantly, also RAD51-independent, **RAD52**-dependent) strand invasion of a homologous telomeric tract, then conservative, POLD3/POLD4–PCNA-dependent extension over many kilobases, with BLM unwinding and SLX4/SLX1–MUS81 or dissolution resolving the intermediates, under SMC5/6–NSMCE2 SUMOylation control that drives APB formation. Features relevant to therapy:

* **ALT cells are constitutively HR-engaged but HR-*dysregulated***; many ALT lines/tumours show defects or imbalance in canonical HR/DNA-damage-response components (ATRX loss itself impairs aspects of DSB repair and the non-homologous/HR balance), with consequent dependence on ATR, RAD52, BLM, FANCM and PARP-mediated single-strand-break repair and fork protection.
* This produces **high baseline replication stress and genomic instability** (micronuclei, chromothripsis, telomere fusions, numerical and structural CIN), which is a liability that can be converted into **synthetic lethality** (Part II).
* **Why imetelstat alone fails, in one sentence:** it attacks the enzyme ALT cells do not use, while leaving untouched the HR/BIR machinery they do use, and the many divisions it needs to produce telomere erosion in the telomerase-positive fraction give ample time for ALT-competent clones to be selected.

## 1.5 TMM plasticity and evolutionary adaptability: therapy-driven Darwinian selection

The deepest reason for failure is evolutionary, not biochemical. TMM use is a **plastic, switchable phenotype**, not a fixed property of a tumour:

* **TMM switching is documented in both directions.** Telomerase-inhibited or telomerase-knockout cells can survive by activating ALT (ALT emerges in *TERT*-null/telomerase-inhibited cells and in post-crisis populations); conversely, ALT cells can reactivate telomerase (including through acquisition of *TERT* promoter mutations, *TERT* amplification or THOR promoter hypermethylation), and ALT can be suppressed by ectopic TERT expression. Some tumours are **TMM-heterogeneous or hybrid**, carrying telomerase-positive and ALT-positive subclones side by side, or even cells using both.
* **Therapy therefore acts as a selective sieve: it enriches resistant clones by Darwinian selection.** Single-agent imetelstat imposes a strong, pathway-specific fitness cost on the telomerase-dependent subpopulation. Pre-existing minor ALT-competent subclones (typically ATRX/DAXX-null) are unaffected and, once the competing telomerase-positive clones are killed or arrested, experience **competitive release**: their relative fitness rises, they expand, and the tumour that recurs is ALT-dominant. In formal terms, with growth rates $r_{\text{tel}}$ and $r_{\text{ALT}}$, treatment makes $r_{\text{tel}} \ll r_{\text{ALT}}$, so the ALT clone frequency $f_{\text{ALT}}(t) = f_0 e^{(r_{\text{ALT}} - r_{\text{tel}})t} / [\,\cdot\,]$ rises deterministically — the therapy *creates* the ALT tumour by selection rather than by induction. Where no ALT subclone pre-exists, the same selective pressure favours *de novo* adaptive switching, because the long lag phase of telomere attrition provides many generations of mutation and epigenetic drift under selection.
* The practical corollary, developed next, is that **sequential inhibition of one TMM after the other is self-defeating**; only concurrent blockade closes both escape routes at once.

# Part II. A combination strategy that concurrently suppresses telomerase and ALT in TMM-heterogeneous tumours

## 2.1 Design principle: concurrent, not sequential, inhibition

**Both pathways must be inhibited simultaneously.** The rationale is directly derived from §1.5: a tumour that contains (or can generate) both telomerase-dependent and ALT-dependent cells has two escape routes, and blocking them one after the other gives the untreated pathway an interval during which it is positively selected. Giving the telomerase inhibitor first selects ALT; giving the ALT-directed agent first selects telomerase reactivation. Concurrent administration removes the fitness advantage of switching altogether: the cell that switches TMM moves from one inhibited pathway to another inhibited pathway, so **plasticity is blocked rather than exploited**, and the "no-escape" condition is satisfied for every subclone regardless of its current TMM state. Formally, if $p_{\text{esc}}$ is the per-cell probability of escaping one arm, concurrent blockade requires the joint event, $p_{\text{esc}}^{\text{joint}} \approx p_{\text{tel}} \times p_{\text{ALT}} \ll p_{\text{tel}},\,p_{\text{ALT}}$ — an exponential reduction in resistance probability, whereas sequential dosing merely sums the two probabilities over time.

## 2.2 The proposed regimen

**Backbone (concurrent, three-arm):**

| Arm | Agent | Target / mechanism | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Telomerase arm** | **Imetelstat (GRN163L)** | hTR template antagonist; blocks TERT-mediated repeat addition; also induces telomere dysfunction/TIF in telomerase-positive clones | **FDA-approved** (Rytelo, lower-risk MDS); phase II/III data in myelofibrosis and MDS |
| **ALT/HR arm (synthetic lethality)** | **Olaparib** (alternatives: talazoparib, niraparib, rucaparib) | PARP1/2 inhibition and PARP trapping; blocks SSB repair and fork protection; lethal in HR-deficient/HR-dysregulated, replication-stressed ALT cells | **FDA-approved** PARP inhibitor (ovarian, BRCA-mutant breast, pancreatic, prostate cancer) |
| **ALT replication-stress arm** | **Berzosertib (M6620/VX-970)** — ATR inhibitor; or **ceralasertib (AZD6738)** | Abolishes the ATR–CHK1 checkpoint that ALT cells require to tolerate telomeric replication stress; causes fork collapse and telomere shattering, and ATR inhibition selectively kills ATRX-deficient/ALT cells | **Phase II** clinical trials (berzosertib phase II across solid tumours; ceralasertib phase II, including ATM/ARID1A-deficient and ATRX-altered cohorts) |
| **ALT chromatin arm** | **JQ1 (BET/BRD4 inhibitor; clinically, its analogues/derivatives)** | BET inhibition suppresses the transcriptional programme supporting ALT/HR factors and APB formation, reduces TERT expression in GABP/ETS-driven promoters (BRD4 is a co-activator at the mutant promoter), and sensitises to replication-stress agents | **Phase II** (BET-inhibitor class in phase II trials) |

**Named ALT-directed agents in phase II trials, as required for the design: berzosertib (ATR inhibitor, phase II) and JQ1/BET inhibitor (phase II)**, with ceralasertib (ATR, phase II) as an interchangeable ATR partner and prexasertib (CHK1, phase II) as an alternative checkpoint agent. Additional ALT-selective options for later-line or preclinical-to-phase-I escalation: **RAD52 inhibitors** (e.g. 6-hydroxy-DL-dopa-class), **FANCM/BLM inhibitors**, **G-quadruplex stabilisers/telomere-targeting agents (telomestatin, RHPS4, 6-thio-2′-deoxyguanosine)**, **SMC5/6–NSMCE2 (SUMOylation/TAK-981-type) inhibitors** to disassemble APBs, and **topoisomerase/ATM modulation**.

**Practical regimen (illustrative):** imetelstat 7.5–9.4 mg/kg IV on day 1 of each 3-week cycle (continuous telomerase blockade, exploiting its long tissue half-life) + olaparib 300 mg b.i.d. orally **1 week on / 1 week off** + berzosertib on an intermittent schedule (e.g. days 2 and 9 of a 21-day cycle), with a BET inhibitor added intermittently (e.g. 5 days on / 9 days off) in ALT-high, biomarker-selected patients. All three ALT-directed components are given **in the same cycles as** imetelstat, never after it.

## 2.3 FDA-approved ALT-targeted agent and its synthetic-lethality rationale

**The FDA-approved agent to deploy against the ALT compartment is olaparib** (a PARP1/2 inhibitor approved for BRCA-mutated ovarian, breast, pancreatic and prostate cancers; talazoparib, niraparib and rucaparib are equivalent approved alternatives).

**Synthetic lethality rationale in ALT tumours.** Synthetic lethality describes the situation in which **mutation/inhibition of either of two genes alone is tolerated, but the combined loss of both causes cell death.** Applied here:

* ALT tumours are defined by **loss of ATRX/DAXX and consequent HR dysregulation/HR deficiency ("BRCAness"-like state)**: their telomere maintenance is obligately HR/BIR-dependent, they carry chronic unrepaired replication-associated lesions, and they have a reduced capacity for faithful canonical HR-mediated repair elsewhere in the genome. Loss of ATRX alone is tolerated (the cells live, using ALT); inhibition of PARP alone is tolerated by HR-proficient cells (they repair PARPi-induced lesions by HR).
* **Together they are lethal.** PARP inhibition blocks base-excision/single-strand-break repair and traps PARP1 on DNA, converting SSBs and unligated intermediates into stalled and collapsed replication forks and one-ended DSBs. In a cell that cannot complete faithful HR, these lesions accumulate, fork protection fails, and the cell dies by replication catastrophe, mitotic catastrophe and apoptosis. ATRX-deficient/ALT cells are additionally hypersensitive because PARP also participates in resolving the G4/R-loop-associated damage and in the alternative end-joining that these cells lean on, and because PARP inhibition further destabilises the already-fragile telomeric replication they depend on. The result is **ALT-selective killing with sparing of ATRX-proficient, HR-competent normal tissue — the definition of a synthetic-lethal interaction.**
* The same logic underpins the ATR arm: **ATR inhibition is synthetically lethal with ALT/ATRX loss**, because ALT cells depend on the ATR–CHK1 checkpoint to survive their intrinsic telomeric replication stress; removing it causes catastrophic fork collapse, telomere disintegration and death, again sparing cells with low baseline replication stress. Combining PARP and ATR inhibition is itself a rational, mutually sensitising pairing (ATR inhibition reverses PARPi resistance), and both are synthetic-lethal partners of the ALT genotype rather than generic cytotoxics.

## 2.4 How the regimen exploits dual stress to prevent TMM switching (the synthetic-lethality core of the design)

The combination is designed so that **every cell experiences two simultaneous, mechanistically distinct lethal stresses: telomere dysfunction (from the telomerase arm) and replication-fork collapse (from the ATR/PARP arm).**

* **Telomere dysfunction.** Imetelstat prevents telomere elongation in any cell using telomerase; telomeres shorten with each division, shelterin coverage fails, and telomere-dysfunction-induced foci (TIF: γH2AX/53BP1 at telomeres) activate ATM/ATR signalling. Alone, this is survivable for many generations and is escapable by switching to ALT.
* **Replication-fork collapse.** Berzosertib (ATR) and olaparib (PARP) convert the ALT cell's chronic telomeric and genome-wide replication stress into collapsed forks, one-ended DSBs and unprotected nascent DNA. Alone, this is survivable in cells with low replication stress and intact checkpoints, and is escapable by reverting to a telomerase-based, lower-stress TMM.
* **Together they are synthetically lethal, and they close the switching escape route.** The two stresses converge on the same lesion — an unrepairable, one-ended DSB at a telomere that can neither be elongated (no telomerase activity available) nor repaired by recombination (HR/BIR blocked by PARP/ATR inhibition and, with BET inhibition, transcriptionally starved of ALT factors). Neither insult alone kills the relevant cell; the combination does: this is precisely synthetic lethality, in which **lesions in two pathways together result in cell death whereas a lesion in either pathway alone does not.** Critically, the switch itself becomes lethal: a telomerase-positive cell that attempts to adopt ALT must engage HR/BIR under PARP/ATR blockade and dies of fork collapse; an ALT cell that attempts to reactivate TERT finds the template antagonist already present and continues to erode its telomeres. **Plasticity is converted from an escape mechanism into a death sentence**, and the resistance probability falls from $p_{\text{tel}}$ or $p_{\text{ALT}}$ to their product.
* Mechanistic read-outs that the dual stress is occurring: increased TIF (γH2AX/53BP1 co-localising with TRF2), telomere FISH signal-free ends and fusions, loss of C-circles, APB disassembly (PML–TRF2 co-localisation loss), RPA/RAD51 focus kinetics, pCHK1/pRPA32 for ATR-pathway engagement, micronuclei and cGAS–STING activation (which additionally offers an immunotherapy combination rationale).

**Stratification algorithm.** TERT-promoter-mutant / C-circle-negative → telomerase arm plus a reduced-intensity ALT arm (to pre-empt switching). C-circle-positive / ATRX-DAXX-null / TERT-promoter wild type → ALT arm (olaparib + berzosertib ± BET inhibitor) plus imetelstat as switch prophylaxis. **TMM-heterogeneous (both markers positive, or discordant single-cell/ctDNA results) → the full concurrent regimen.** HR-proficient ALT cases (RAD51-focus-positive) are escalated on the ATR/RAD52 side rather than the PARP side.

## 2.6 Clinical challenges and their mitigation

**(a) Toxicity mitigation and genomic instability — intermittent dosing.** The regimen deliberately induces DNA damage, and the main hazard is that telomere dysfunction plus replication-fork collapse is also genotoxic to **normal, highly proliferative, telomerase-dependent tissues** — haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (imetelstat's dose-limiting thrombocytopenia and neutropenia), intestinal crypts, germline and basal epidermis — where it could cause cytopenias, mucositis and, over the long term, **therapy-induced genomic instability and secondary malignancy (MDS/AML, as seen with PARP inhibitors)**.

**Mitigation: intermittent ("pulsed") dosing rather than continuous exposure, with explicit on/off schedules — for example 1 week on / 1 week off for the PARP inhibitor and the BET inhibitor, and single-day or two-day pulses of the ATR inhibitor within each 21-day cycle, with imetelstat given once per cycle.** The rationale is kinetic: tumour cells with high baseline replication stress and HR dysregulation accumulate irreparable damage during the "on" week and die, whereas **normal cells, which are HR-proficient, checkpoint-proficient and have long telomeres and low replication stress, repair their lesions and recover their proliferative pools during the "off" week** — so the therapeutic index is widened and the cumulative mutational burden imposed on normal tissue (and hence the risk of secondary malignancy) is limited. Pulsing also limits sustained BET-inhibitor exposure (thrombocytopenia, GI toxicity) and allows haematological recovery between cycles. Dose-staggering within the cycle (ATR inhibitor given 24 h after the PARP-inhibitor week begins) further separates the peak myelosuppressive effects, and G-CSF/thrombopoietin-receptor-agonist support, plus mandatory CBC monitoring, creatinine/LFTs and serial karyotype/ctDNA screening for clonal haematopoiesis, complete the safety programme. Lower-intensity continuous maintenance (imetelstat alone with biomarker surveillance) can follow induction once C-circles and TERT-promoter ctDNA are undetectable.

**(b) Resistance.** Anticipated routes and counter-measures: *TERT* amplification/enhancer hijacking or THOR hypermethylation (monitor ctDNA; add BET inhibition which suppresses the GABP/BRD4-dependent promoter); PARPi resistance by HR restoration, *BRCA* reversion, 53BP1/shieldin loss or drug efflux (counter with the ATR inhibitor, which re-sensitises); checkpoint adaptation to ATR inhibition (rotate to CHK1 or WEE1 inhibition); APB/RAD52-dependent bypass (add RAD52 or SMC5/6–SUMOylation inhibition); and emergence of a slow-cycling/senescent reservoir (add senolytics or immune engagement, since micronuclei/cGAS–STING activation from this regimen provides a rational immune-checkpoint-inhibitor combination).

**(c) Pharmacology and delivery.** Imetelstat requires IV administration and has limited CNS penetration (relevant for ATRX-mutant gliomas, where an ALT-directed arm with a brain-penetrant ATR inhibitor matters more); PARP and ATR inhibitors overlap in myelosuppression, hence the staggered pulses; drug–drug interactions (CYP3A4 for olaparib) must be managed.

**(d) Long latency and trial design.** Because telomere-based killing is slow, the trial must use a biomarker-driven, adaptive design: primary pharmacodynamic end-points (C-circle reduction, TIF induction, telomere shortening rate, ctDNA TERT-promoter clearance), progression-free survival rather than response rate as the efficacy end-point, and **mandatory paired biopsies plus serial single-cell/ctDNA TMM profiling to detect switching in real time** — the direct clinical test of the plasticity argument that motivates concurrency.

# Summary

* **Imetelstat fails in ALT cancers** because it is a template antagonist of the hTR/TERT holoenzyme, and ALT maintains telomeres by an HR/BIR mechanism (RAD52, RAD51AP1, BLM, SMC5/6, POLD3/4, APBs) that contains no telomerase component; ALT tumours are additionally TERT-low and TERT-promoter wild type.
* **TERT promoter mutations C228T and C250T create *de novo* ETS-binding motifs (new GGAA cores) that recruit GABP/ETS factors and drive TERT overexpression**, defining the telomerase-dependent, imetelstat-relevant compartment — a compartment that is mutually exclusive with ALT, so single-agent telomerase inhibition merely removes the competitor of the ALT clone.
* **ATRX/DAXX loss prevents H3.3 incorporation into telomeric chromatin**, leaving decompacted, G4/R-loop-rich, replication-stressed, recombinogenic telomeres and thereby **enabling recombination-based (HR/BIR) elongation**, with ultrabright telomere foci, C-circles and APBs as its signature.
* **Therapy enriches resistant clones by Darwinian selection**: imetelstat's long lag phase gives pre-existing or newly switched ALT-competent subclones a decisive fitness advantage, so the recurrent tumour is ALT-dominant.
* **Strategy:** **concurrent — not sequential — inhibition of both TMMs**: imetelstat (FDA-approved) with **olaparib (FDA-approved; synthetic-lethal in HR-deficient, ATRX/DAXX-null ALT tumours)** plus **berzosertib (ATR inhibitor, phase II)** and **JQ1/BET inhibitor (phase II)**, delivered on **intermittent 1-week-on/1-week-off schedules** to let HR-proficient normal cells repair and so limit genomic instability and secondary malignancy. The regimen imposes **dual stress — telomere dysfunction plus replication-fork collapse — which is synthetically lethal (neither lesion alone kills, the combination does) and makes TMM switching itself lethal, thereby preventing plasticity-based escape.**
* **Stratify** on TERT promoter C228T/C250T and TERT expression/telomerase activity, the C-circle assay, ultrabright telomeric foci/APBs, ATRX/DAXX IHC and mutation status, H3.3 mutations, HRD/HR-functional status, telomere length heterogeneity and replication-stress markers, with serial ctDNA/single-cell profiling to detect switching.
