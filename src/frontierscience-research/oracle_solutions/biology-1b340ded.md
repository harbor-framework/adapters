# A theoretical framework for a new model research system for abiotic × biotic stress crosstalk in an edible crop

## 1. Design logic: the three constraints that determine everything else

Stress-crosstalk biology is intrinsically combinatorial. If a system contains $n$ stressors applied at $k$ intensities and $t$ timings, the design space scales as $\sim (k \cdot t)^n$; with interaction terms, the number of pairwise comparisons grows as $\binom{n}{2}$ and the number of distinguishable response classes grows faster still. Add the fact that abiotic and biotic responses are antagonistically wired at the hormone level (ABA ↔ SA/JA/ET crosstalk, shared MAPK cascades, shared ROS and Ca²⁺ second messengers, shared WRKY/NAC/MYB transcription factors), and the practical conclusion is unavoidable:

**Constraint 1 — the model research system must be established using exactly one discrete abiotic stress and one discrete biotic stress, and no more.** Because the nature of crosstalk stress studies is inherently complex, **one abiotic plus one biotic stress is simultaneously the minimum and the maximum needed**: it is the minimum because crosstalk cannot exist with fewer than two stresses of different type, and it is the maximum because every additional stressor multiplies the factorial design, confounds attribution of any transcriptional or metabolic change to a specific interaction, and destroys the statistical power needed to detect interaction terms. Only after the two-stress system is fully characterised and validated should a third factor ever be contemplated.

**Constraint 2 — both chosen stresses must be genuinely relevant, i.e. each must pose a serious, quantifiable threat to food security.** There is no value in a tractable system built on a trivial stress. Acceptable abiotic choices are therefore of the salinity/drought class (soil salinisation already affects >400 Mha and ~20–33% of irrigated land, and is expanding by ~1–2 Mha yr⁻¹; drought and salinity together cause the largest share of global yield loss to abiotic factors). Acceptable biotic choices are of the crop-disease/major-pest-pressure class (pests and pathogens remove ~20–40% of global crop production annually; bacterial diseases alone can cause 50–70% yield losses in epidemic years). Both chosen stresses must sit in these categories so that any trait discovered has direct translational value.

**Constraint 3 — the plant must be a crop, not a non-edible model.** The entire motivation is translation, so the chassis must be a species people actually grow and eat (criteria in §2).

## 2. Choice of model plant: **tomato, *Solanum lycopersicum***

I propose **cultivated tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*)** as the chassis — reference cultivar **'Heinz 1706'** for genomics and the dwarf, rapid-cycling **'Micro-Tom'** as the laboratory workhorse, with elite field cultivars (e.g. 'M82', 'Moneymaker', and locally adapted processing lines) for scale-up. It satisfies every requirement for a new multi-stress model research system:

1. **It is a crop species.** Tomato is a true agricultural crop (~190 Mt produced annually across >5 Mha in >170 countries, the world's highest-value vegetable crop), not a non-edible laboratory model such as *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Everything learned is immediately about an edible product, and tomato simultaneously functions as a genetic model (short generation time of ~3 months, self-pollinating, high seed number, efficient *Agrobacterium* transformation, CRISPR/Cas9 routinely applied, grafting-amenable), so it uniquely combines crop status with model-organism tractability.
2. **A high-quality genome sequence is available.** The *S. lycopersicum* 'Heinz 1706' genome (~900 Mb, 12 chromosomes, ~35,000 genes) has been published and iteratively improved to chromosome-scale, telomere-to-telomere assemblies; pan-genomes of hundreds of accessions, wild relatives (*S. pimpinellifolium*, *S. pennellii*, *S. chilense*) and introgression-line libraries, plus extensive annotation, resequencing panels, eQTL maps and a tomato pan-genome graph, are public. This is non-negotiable for a crosstalk system, because the read-outs are transcriptome-, epigenome- and GWAS-based and require a reference.
3. **It has high nutritional value.** Tomato is a major dietary source of lycopene and β-carotene, vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, potassium and phenolic antioxidants, and is the single largest vegetable contributor of these to many national diets. Improving its resilience therefore protects micronutrient supply, not only calories — which matters for food *and* nutrition security.
4. **It already has a substantial level of tolerance to one of the two chosen stresses — salinity.** Tomato is among the more salt-tolerant vegetable crops (threshold ~2.5 dS m⁻¹, with cultivars and rootstocks productive at 40–80 mM NaCl and measurable yield into the 100–150 mM range), it naturally grows in semi-arid Mediterranean and coastal production zones, and highly salt-tolerant wild relatives (*S. pimpinellifolium*, *S. pennellii*, *S. chilense*, *S. galapagense*) provide donor alleles. This pre-existing tolerance is a deliberate design choice, not an accident: **a crop that already copes with the abiotic stressor survives long enough under that stress to mount, and to be measured mounting, a full immune response.** A salt-sensitive species would simply die or collapse before the biotic stress could be imposed, truncating the response window and confounding crosstalk phenotypes with general senescence. Starting from an already-tolerant background therefore **ensures a far greater scope for identifying valuable multi-stress crosstalk traits** — a wide dynamic range of viable phenotypes in which the interaction (synergistic, antagonistic or additive) can actually be resolved, and a realistic baseline from which further gains are translationally meaningful.
5. **It is socially relevant — already farmed and consumed by a very large population.** Tomato is produced and eaten on every inhabited continent, in smallholder and industrial systems alike, is central to the diets of billions, and supports large processing, export and smallholder-income sectors. Improvements therefore reach an existing value chain with existing seed systems, extension services and consumer acceptance — no new crop has to be introduced for the research to have impact.
6. **Reliable pre-existing datasets from previous single-stress studies exist.** There is a deep, curated literature and public data resource for tomato under salinity alone (ion-exclusion and Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis genes *SlHKT1;1/1;2*, *SlSOS1*, *SlNHX*, ABA biosynthesis *SlNCED1*, *SlAREB1*, osmolyte and antioxidant responses; salinity QTL and transcriptome/metabolome time courses) and under pathogen attack alone (*Pto/Prf*, *Fen*, *Mi-1*, *SlMAPK3*, *SlNPR1*, *SlEDS1*, PR-gene and SA/JA marker panels; full transcriptomes of DC3000-infected tomato). These datasets are the indispensable baseline against which combined-stress responses are compared, and they let the new system be benchmarked rather than built from zero.
7. **Pre-established outdoor field infrastructure exists for larger-scale trials.** Tomato is grown in replicated field trials, screenhouses and commercial-standard plots worldwide (e.g. UC Davis and the California processing-tomato system, Mediterranean and Chinese production regions, AVRDC/World Vegetable Center, EU and CGIAR-linked stations), including in **naturally saline and coastal soils** and in regions with endemic bacterial disease pressure. Validated laboratory/controlled-environment findings can therefore be moved directly into multi-site, multi-season field trials with standard agronomic and yield protocols.
8. **Hence direct transfer of knowledge to food security.** Because the chassis is an edible, widely farmed, nutritionally valuable crop with a genome, prior single-stress data and field capacity, every step of the pipeline — gene discovery → engineered/bred line → controlled-environment validation → field trial → variety release — can be completed within one system, without the species change that normally breaks translation from *Arabidopsis* to agriculture.

*(Equally defensible alternative chassis, by the same criteria: rice (*Oryza sativa*), with the salt-tolerant landrace Pokkali/FL478 background, *Xanthomonas oryzae* pv. *oryzae* as the pathogen and IRRI field infrastructure; or barley. Tomato is preferred here because it combines crop status, nutritional density, salinity tolerance, genome quality and a canonical model bacterial pathogen in one species.)*

## 3. Choice of the two stresses

### 3.1 Abiotic stressor: **salt stress (NaCl)**

Salinity is the correct single abiotic stressor for the following reasons:

* **Ease of administration and precise control.** Salt is simply added to the growth medium as a defined molar concentration of NaCl, is chemically stable, does not degrade or evaporate, and can be dosed, measured and verified continuously (electrical conductivity in dS m⁻¹, Na⁺/Cl⁻ analysis, osmotic potential). Unlike drought — where the stress intensity is a moving target that depends on soil texture, pot size, transpiration rate, vapour-pressure deficit and watering history, and is therefore notoriously hard to replicate between labs — salinity can be held at an exactly known, constant, reproducible intensity, which is the single most important property for a crosstalk system where two stress intensities must be varied independently.
* **A wealth of pre-existing molecular-level data.** Salt stress is among the best-dissected abiotic stresses at the mechanistic level: the SOS1/SOS2/SOS3 pathway, HKT transporters, NHX vacuolar antiporters, ABA biosynthesis and signalling (NCED, PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2, AREB/ABF), Ca²⁺ and ROS signatures, osmolyte metabolism, and extensive transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic/ionomic reference datasets in tomato and across species. A new system should be built where the single-stress molecular baseline is already solid, so that the *interaction* is the only unknown.
* **Ease of evaluating salt-stressed phenotypes.** Salinity produces well-defined, quantitative, non-destructive phenotypes on a known two-phase timeline (rapid osmotic phase, then slower ionic/toxic phase): shoot and root biomass, relative growth rate, leaf area, stomatal conductance, chlorophyll fluorescence ($F_v/F_m$), relative water content, leaf Na⁺/K⁺ ratio, proline/MDA, leaf necrosis scores and yield. These scales are standardised, continuous and reproducible, so dose–response curves can be constructed reliably — which is exactly what is needed before combining stresses.
* **It is a first-order food-security threat** (see Constraint 2), and it is spatially predictable, so field validation sites in naturally saline soils already exist.

### 3.2 Biotic stressor: **a model bacterial pathogen — *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000** (alternative: *Xanthomonas*, e.g. *X. euvesicatoria/perforans* on tomato or *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzae* on rice)

A **model pathogen species** must be used, and it should be a bacterium, chosen as follows:

* **High prevalence and major risk of crop-limiting disease.** *P. syringae* is one of the most widespread and damaging bacterial plant pathogens globally; *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* causes bacterial speck, and the related *Xanthomonas* spp. cause bacterial spot, with field losses that can exceed 50% and no effective curative chemistry. Bacterial disease therefore meets the relevance criterion squarely.
* **Ease and precision of use in experiments — it is a *discrete*, dose-controlled stress.** The inoculum is a defined, quantifiable entity: a single, genome-sequenced, culturable strain, delivered at an exactly specified titre (e.g. OD₆₀₀ → $10^4$–$10^6$ cfu mL⁻¹, or cfu per mL by dilution plating) by a reproducible route (syringe infiltration of a defined leaf area, or dip/spray inoculation with surfactant for a more natural route), at a defined developmental stage and leaf position. Disease progression is quantified objectively as in-planta bacterial titre (cfu cm⁻²) over time, lesion number/area and disease index.
* **Unmatched molecular toolkit.** DC3000 offers isogenic mutant sets that let the *immune* layer be dissected within the crosstalk experiment: *hrcC⁻* (no type-III secretion → PTI only), *ΔavrPto/ΔavrPtoB* (ETI through Pto/Prf), *ΔcorR*/coronatine mutants (JA-mimic-dependent stomatal and systemic effects), plus luminescent/fluorescent reporter derivatives. It also provides a well-mapped, species-matched host response in tomato (SA/PR1, JA/ET, *SlMAPK3*, *SlNPR1*, stomatal immunity), and tomato–DC3000 is a genuine, ecologically real host–pathogen pair, not an artificial one.
* **It is a single, discrete biotic agent**, so the biotic axis has one dimension and one dose variable — satisfying Constraint 1.

### 3.3 Why *not* biological pests, and why *not* artificial biotic stresses (PAMPs/elicitors)

* **Insect herbivores and other biological pests are not valid choices here.** Living pests introduce their own behaviour, development, feeding-site choice, population dynamics, microbiota and salt-sensitivity, so the applied dose is not controllable: the "stress intensity" depends on insect behaviour that itself responds to the plant's salt status (a confounded, non-independent factor). Adding that variance to a research system that is already inherently complex makes interaction terms uninterpretable — the control required is simply unattainable at the establishment stage of the model.
* **Artificial biotic stresses (purified PAMPs/MAMPs such as flg22, chitin, elf18, or elicitor sprays) are also not valid choices.** They are convenient and reproducible, but they are not a disease: they trigger a partial, synchronised, single-receptor signalling event without colonisation, effector delivery, nutrient withdrawal or tissue damage, and they produce no yield-relevant disease phenotype. Any result obtained with them requires **an extra translational step** — re-validation with a real pathogen — before it can inform food security, which defeats the purpose of building a translational system. PAMPs belong in the system later, as mechanistic probes within the validated framework, not as the biotic stressor that defines it.

## 4. Growing method: a precise, controlled regime for administering both stresses

**Phase A — controlled environment with hydroponics for the abiotic axis.** Plants are first grown under carefully controlled conditions in a **hydroponic growth system** (deep-water culture, nutrient film technique or aerated Hoagland/half-strength Hoagland solution; rockwool or inert perlite plugs for support) in a walk-in growth chamber or phytotron with fixed photoperiod (e.g. 16 h light/8 h dark), fixed PPFD (~200–400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), fixed temperature (24 °C day/18 °C night), 60–70% RH and controlled CO₂. **Salt stress is administered by adjusting the NaCl concentration of the nutrient solution across a defined series — e.g. 0 (control), 25, 50, 75, 100, 150 mM NaCl — verified continuously by electrical conductivity, pH, osmolality and ionic analysis, with solutions replaced on a fixed schedule and NaCl stepped in gradually (e.g. 25 mM d⁻¹) to avoid osmotic shock artefacts.** Hydroponics is specified deliberately: it removes soil heterogeneity, gives every root system an identical, instantly adjustable and exactly measurable ionic environment, allows root sampling without disturbance, and makes the applied stress intensity a true independent variable. (A matched soil/pot and, later, a saline-field arm is run in parallel for agronomic realism, because hydroponics changes root architecture and aeration.)

**Phase B — inoculation for the biotic axis.** The pathogen is applied to the same plants in the same chamber at a defined leaf stage (e.g. leaves 3–4 of 5-week-old plants), by syringe infiltration of a marked leaf area with a defined titre (e.g. $10^5$ cfu mL⁻¹ in 10 mM MgCl₂) for mechanistic precision, and/or by dip/spray inoculation with 0.02% Silwet L-77 followed by 24 h at high humidity for a natural infection route. Mock inoculation (10 mM MgCl₂ ± surfactant) is the matched control. Bacterial growth is scored at 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 dpi by tissue maceration and dilution plating (log cfu cm⁻²).

**Randomisation and replication.** Full factorial blocks, randomised positions with regular rotation to control chamber microclimate gradients, ≥6 biological replicates per cell for phenotypes and ≥3–4 for omics, independent experimental repeats, and chamber-to-chamber replication.

## 5. Order of experiments when establishing the system

The framework's experimental order is itself a result, because it determines what can be interpreted later.

### Stage 1 — Characterise the unstressed baseline and the single-stress dose–response curves

**The logical starting point is to establish control (un-stressed) versus stressed phenotypes using a range of stress intensities of singly applied stressors only.** Explicitly: before any combination is attempted, the **un-stressed control phenotype must be characterised in full** — growth rate, leaf area, biomass, ion content, stomatal conductance, $F_v/F_m$, baseline hormone levels, baseline transcriptome/metabolome and baseline disease susceptibility — because every subsequent interaction is defined as a deviation from this baseline, and without a quantified control there is no scale on which to measure synergy or antagonism. Then:

* **Salt alone**, across the 0/25/50/75/100/150 mM series, scoring the phenotypes in §3.1 over time, to define: the no-effect threshold, the sub-lethal "moderate" level that reduces growth by ~20–30% without senescence (the level most useful for crosstalk), and the severe/lethal level.
* **Pathogen alone**, across an inoculum series ($10^3$–$10^6$ cfu mL⁻¹), scoring in-planta titre, lesion area and disease index, to define low, moderate (clear but sub-maximal disease) and high disease pressure.

These single-stress response surfaces are what **guide every subsequent combined-stress experiment**: they supply the matched intensities, the sampling times, the marker-gene panels and the expected additive null model $E_{\text{additive}}$ against which the observed combined response is tested for interaction ($E_{\text{obs}} \ne E_{\text{additive}} \Rightarrow$ crosstalk). They also benchmark the new system against the pre-existing single-stress literature (criterion 6 above), confirming the platform behaves as published before anything novel is claimed.

### Stage 2 — Decide, and justify, which stress is applied first

It makes sense to **begin with the abiotic stressor and apply it continuously, then superimpose the biotic stress as an acute event.** The rationale is ecological fidelity:

* **Salinity is a chronic, continuously applied property of the soil/plant growth medium.** A plant in a saline field is salt-stressed from germination onwards and never escapes it. Delivering NaCl continuously through the hydroponic medium and **establishing steady growth under saline conditions simulates how plants actually grow in naturally saline soils**, with acclimated ion homeostasis, acclimated ABA tone and acclimated growth rate — the physiological state that a real crop is in when a pathogen arrives.
* **Pathogen infection, by contrast, is an acute, stochastic event that can easily occur suddenly** at any point in the season — an inoculum arrives with rain splash, wind, insect vectors, irrigation water or contaminated tools and colonises an already-acclimated plant within hours to days. The realistic sequence is therefore *chronic abiotic background → sudden biotic challenge*.
* There is also a methodological reason: the continuously applied stress can be held at a verified steady state, so the plant's physiological status at the moment of inoculation is known and uniform, which makes the acute biotic response interpretable. The reciprocal order (infect, then salinise) and the simultaneous application are run later as deliberate comparisons to test whether crosstalk is order-dependent (priming vs. antagonism), but they are not the order in which the system is established.

### Stage 3 — Apply simple combinations of the two stressors

**After a range of stress phenotypes has been established for the singly applied stresses, the next logical step is to apply simple combinations of the stressors** — pairwise, two-factor combinations only, starting at the moderate intensities identified in Stage 1. Concretely:

* **Grow the plants under the abiotic stressor first and then apply the biotic stress.** For example: **stress the plants with salt for a minimum of one week (7–14 days at 50–75 mM NaCl), so that salt acclimation is established and verified (growth rate, leaf Na⁺/K⁺, ABA, salt-marker transcripts), and only then inoculate with *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000** at the defined titre; score disease and plant phenotypes at 0–4 dpi against the four-way control set (mock/no-salt, mock/salt, inoculated/no-salt, inoculated/salt).
* **The next step is then to determine the appropriate intensities/concentrations for the dual-stress application** — i.e. a two-dimensional dose matrix (NaCl × inoculum: e.g. 0/50/100 mM × $10^4$/$10^5$/$10^6$ cfu mL⁻¹), plus a timing series (salt for 7 vs 14 vs 21 days before inoculation). The goal is to identify the combination window in which both stresses produce a measurable, sub-lethal phenotype simultaneously, since only in that window can interactions be quantified; outside it one stress masks the other or the plant dies.
* Only within that validated window are molecular read-outs deployed: time-resolved RNA-seq (with proper interaction-term modelling, e.g. a $\sim$ salt × pathogen × time design in DESeq2/limma), hormone quantification (ABA, SA, JA-Ile, ET) by LC-MS/MS, ROS and Ca²⁺ imaging, single-cell/spatial transcriptomics of infected versus uninfected tissue, metabolomics, cell-wall composition, and ChIP/ATAC for shared promoter elements. The analytical target is the set of genes and metabolites whose combined-stress response is **not** predicted by the sum of the single-stress responses — the crosstalk nodes (candidate classes: ABA–SA antagonism, shared MAPKs, WRKY/NAC/MYB hubs, stomatal control, ROS/redox, cell-wall remodelling, osmolyte/secondary-metabolite flux).
* Validation: candidate nodes are tested by CRISPR knockouts, overexpression lines, VIGS, natural-variation panels/GWAS across tomato accessions and introgression lines from salt-tolerant wild relatives, and grafting to separate root- from shoot-derived signals.

## 6. Applied science outputs: turning the controlled system into food security gains

The controlled experimental system is explicitly designed to feed an applied pipeline, and the applied outputs are achieved by **genetic engineering (CRISPR/Cas9 knock-in, promoter editing, cisgenesis, overexpression under stress-inducible or tissue-specific promoters) and/or conventional and marker-assisted breeding** (introgression from salt-tolerant wild relatives, genomic selection on the crosstalk loci, pyramiding of favourable haplotypes identified by GWAS):

* **The central applied goal is to enhance the crop's multi-stress tolerance by exploiting the crosstalk itself — specifically, to enhance the crop's disease tolerance by targeting and upregulating abiotic (salt) stress defence pathways that overlap with, or interact positively with, immune pathways.** The crosstalk screen in §5 identifies exactly those shared nodes where an abiotic-defence output also strengthens defence against the pathogen (and ideally avoids the classic ABA–SA antagonism that normally makes salt-stressed plants more susceptible). Engineering or selecting for elevated/constitutive activity of such a node delivers tolerance to both stresses from a single intervention, rather than stacking two independent traits.
* **Concrete examples of what such a dual-benefit node looks like:**
  1. **Enhanced physical barriers to pathogens through reinforced/thicker cell walls.** Salt stress induces cell-wall remodelling (lignification, suberisation, callose, cross-linked extensins, pectin methylesterification changes, cellulose synthase-like activity). Up-regulating these same pathways — e.g. editing promoters of *CesA*/*Csl*, *PMEI*, *PAL*/*CCR*/*CAD* lignin genes or callose synthase *GSL5/PMR4*, or selecting haplotypes with higher basal lignin/callose — simultaneously limits Na⁺ apoplastic bypass flow and water loss *and* creates a thicker, less penetrable barrier and faster papilla formation against bacterial colonisation.
  2. **Enhanced, faster and stronger stress-hormone signalling.** Both axes converge on ABA, SA, JA and ET signalling and on shared MAPK cascades. Engineering faster, stronger and better-coordinated signalling — e.g. tuning *SlMAPK3*, SnRK2/PP2C components, *SlNPR1*, *SlAREB1*, or WRKY/NAC hubs with stress-inducible rather than constitutive promoters, or editing the ABA–SA antagonism node so that salt acclimation no longer suppresses SA-dependent immunity — gives a plant that mounts a quicker, higher-amplitude defence to either stress and does not trade one off against the other. Primed/"faster" signalling is preferred to constitutive activation because it avoids the growth penalty.
  3. **Higher basal levels of generic stress-protective metabolites and antimicrobial compounds.** Raising the constitutive pool of compounds that serve both functions — osmoprotectants and antioxidants (proline, glycine betaine, polyamines, ascorbate/glutathione, flavonoids and phenylpropanoids), and antimicrobials (tomatine and other glycoalkaloids, phenolics, PR proteins/defensins, ROS-detoxifying and chitinase/glucanase activities) — buys protection against osmotic/ionic damage *and* directly inhibits the pathogen, while flavonoids and phenolics also add nutritional value to the fruit. Promoter editing of the relevant biosynthetic regulators (e.g. MYB transcription factors of the phenylpropanoid pathway) is the obvious route.
* **Deliverables and translation.** From the system come: (i) a validated catalogue of crosstalk genes/alleles and molecular markers for breeding; (ii) edited and bred elite lines carrying one or more of the above dual-benefit traits; (iii) **field validation** — the controlled-environment results are tested in the crop's **pre-established field sites**, including naturally saline soils under endemic bacterial disease pressure, over multiple seasons and locations, with yield, fruit number/size, soluble solids, lycopene/vitamin C content and disease index as end-points, alongside growth-penalty and off-target checks; (iv) agronomic decision tools (e.g. the salinity thresholds at which disease risk changes, informing irrigation water quality and spray/biocontrol scheduling); and (v) seed delivered through existing tomato seed systems to existing growers. Because the chassis is an edible, widely farmed, nutritionally dense crop with prior data, a genome and field infrastructure, this pipeline closes the loop from fundamental crosstalk mechanism to **tangible support for food security** without a change of species.

## 7. The framework in summary

| Element | Decision | Core justification |
|---|---|---|
| Number of stresses | **Exactly one abiotic + one biotic** | Minimum for crosstalk, maximum tractable; crosstalk studies are inherently complex |
| Stress relevance | Salinity; bacterial crop disease | Both are serious, quantified threats to food security |
| Plant | **Tomato (*S. lycopersicum*)** | Crop species; sequenced genome; high nutritional value; already salt-tolerant → greater scope for crosstalk traits; socially relevant and widely consumed; rich single-stress datasets; established field sites → direct translation |
| Abiotic stressor | **NaCl salt stress** | Easy, exact, stable administration; large body of molecular data; easily and reproducibly scored phenotypes |
| Biotic stressor | **Model bacterium *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000** (or *Xanthomonas*) | High prevalence and major crop-limiting disease risk; discrete, dose-controlled, experimentally easy; strong mutant toolkit. **Not** pests (uncontrollable in an already complex system) and **not** PAMPs/artificial elicitors (extra step needed for real-life translatability) |
| Growing method | Controlled-environment **hydroponics with defined NaCl concentrations**, then defined-titre inoculation | Exact, verifiable, independently adjustable stress intensities |
| Order of work | 1. Characterise **control vs. single-stress phenotypes across an intensity range**; 2. apply the **abiotic stress continuously via the growth medium first** (mimics naturally saline soils) because **pathogen infection occurs suddenly**; 3. **simple combinations — salt for ≥1 week, then inoculate**; 4. **determine the appropriate dual-stress intensities/concentrations**; 5. omics/genetics of the interaction | Each stage supplies the baseline the next stage needs |
| Applied output | **Genetic engineering and/or breeding** to enhance multi-stress tolerance by **up-regulating salt-defence pathways that overlap/interact with immune pathways** — thicker/reinforced cell walls, faster/stronger stress-hormone signalling, higher basal protective metabolites and antimicrobials — validated in **multi-site field trials** | Converts crosstalk mechanism into deployable, food-security-relevant varieties |
